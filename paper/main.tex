\noindent{\bfseries Every company records its work in many systems, such as email, chat, customer records and support tickets. Each system holds part of what happens with a customer, but no system holds the situation itself. People rebuild that situation in their heads, and AI assistants rebuild it from documents for each question\citep{lewis2020rag}. A world model of the organization would hold that situation, as world models of physical scenes hold their state\citep{ha2018world,lecun2022path,assran2025vjepa2}. Here we show that the links that enterprise systems keep can train the base of such a model: one state space for a company. A recent theorem predicts that encoders trained to predict one observation of a state from another converge to one representation\citep{balestriero2026platonic}. Records that different systems link to the same customer in the same period are such observations. We test this in a simulated company with exact ground truth\citep{gruenbaum2026era}. There, small encoders trained on these links place 76\% of records with withheld links with the right customer, against 53\% for text similarity. They keep part of this signal when we mask every name. Permissions are part of the model. The same space indexes a memory and a first decision layer, which shows what data a company needs to learn what its decisions do. At this scale, each model trains in minutes on an ordinary computer. A company can thus learn the first layer of a model of itself from its own records.\par}

\medskip
Ask anyone in a company about the current situation of its largest customer. Parts of the answer are in a sales call last Tuesday, in three support tickets and in a chat thread. Other parts are in an email chain with procurement, and in a stage of the customer-relationship management (CRM) system that is two weeks old. Each system records a piece of the situation, but no system records the situation itself. People rebuild it in their heads, slowly and partially, and each person rebuilds it differently. AI assistants rebuild it from scratch for each question.

Enterprise AI gives models knowledge of a company in four main ways (Table~\ref{tab:compare}). Retrieval finds documents for each question, and a language model reads them\citep{lewis2020rag}. Explicit memories keep entities, facts and decisions in a graph or a ledger, with their sources and dates\citep{edge2024graphrag,packer2023memgpt,rasmussen2025zep,chhikara2025mem0}. Compressed memories turn a document collection into a few tokens that a frozen model can read\citep{fallahpour2026memorilla}. Trained approaches study a company's information ahead of time, and compress it into a cache or learn to use notes about it\citep{eyuboglu2025cartridges,engram2026notes}. None of these approaches keeps a learned state of each situation that the records describe.

In robotics and video, such a state is the core of a world model\citep{ha2018world,hafner2020dreamer,lecun2022path}. A world model encodes observations into a latent state, predicts how the state changes under actions, and lets an agent plan\citep{assran2025vjepa2,zhang2026hjepa}. For an organization, the world is the situation of every customer, deal, project and team. The observations are the records that the organization produces as it works. A world model of an organization would map every record, from every system, into one state space. It would then predict how states change, and how decisions change them.

The hard part is to learn such a space without labels, because nobody annotates situations. The Platonic Representation Theorem\citep{huh2024platonic,balestriero2026platonic} says which training signal is enough. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn to predict the embedding of one view of an input from another view\citep{lecun2022path,assran2023ijepa,balestriero2025lejepa}. The theorem calls two different observations of the same latent state \good{}. With good views, such encoders converge to one representation, up to a rotation. With bad views, which corrupt a single observation, they do not.

In most domains, good views are scarce, because someone must pair the observations. Our central observation is that organizations produce good views as a side effect of their work. The same customer appears in an email, a ticket, a call and a CRM update in the same week. The systems record the links themselves: account fields, ticket organizations, channel names and calendar invitations. Records that systems link to one customer in one period observe the same situation, so they are good views that nobody has to label. Earlier work used linked records as positive pairs, for papers that cite each other\citep{cohan2020specter} and for the records of one patient\citep{cai2024ehr} (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:related}).

A model of a company has one more requirement that a model of the physical world does not have: not everyone may see everything. A model that learns from all records can reveal what it learned to anyone who asks. Access control must therefore be part of the model, not a filter on its output.

Here we build and test the first layers of a world model of an organization on a simulated company with exact ground truth\citep{eon2026era,gruenbaum2026era}. Good views from links give one state space for seven systems, which places records with withheld links by their content (Figs.~\ref{fig:idea} and~\ref{fig:state}). We build access control into the model, so that each reader's results depend only on records that the reader may read (Fig.~\ref{fig:acl}). The state space indexes a memory, which finds records by situation and reads them by detail (Fig.~\ref{fig:memory}). Finally, we build a decision layer on the same space, and we identify the data that it needs (Fig.~\ref{fig:decisions}).

\section{Links between systems supply good views}

We model an organization as a set of entities, such as customers, whose situation changes over time (Methods). A state is one entity in one time window, for example one customer in one quarter. Each record is an observation of one state, which a system renders with its own author, template and format. A record belongs to a state when its system links it to the customer and its date is in the window. The encoder never reads these links. The links only decide which records count as views of the same state.

We compare three training objectives on the same text, encoder and budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:idea}). With good views, the views of an anchor record are other records of its state, usually from other systems. With bad views, each view is a damaged copy of the anchor itself, which is the usual self-supervision for text. Next-token prediction learns to predict the next word of each record. We also compare text similarity (TF-IDF), a random encoder and a frozen pretrained sentence encoder\citep{reimers2019sbert,wang2020minilm}. We use the pretrained encoder on its own, and with a small head that we train on good views.

Era by Eon\citep{eon2026era,gruenbaum2026era} generates a company, Northwind Robotics, whose seven systems agree with each other and have exact ground truth. The systems are Gmail, Google Calendar, Slack, Salesforce, Zendesk, Gong and Jira. On the first export date, the company has 78{,}048 records. The systems link 21{,}972 of them to one of 21 active customers, in 180 customer-quarter states (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:data}). We train small encoders from scratch, so that everything that they know comes from this company's records.

\section{One state space for seven systems}

On the records that we use for training, good views gather the records of each customer from every system (Fig.~\ref{fig:state}a--c). Of a record's ten nearest neighbours from other systems, 95.1\% belong to the same customer. With bad views and next-token prediction, it is 13.6\% and 13.2\%, about as low as with a random encoder (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:e1}). In the good-view space, the state explains most of the variance, and the system that wrote a record explains little of it. However, training records are an easy test, because the same customer-quarter groups define the pairs and the score (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:e1}).

The fairer test withholds links. We hid a quarter of the threads, with 5{,}366 records, from training, so that their links never formed a pair. Then we placed each hidden record at the nearest training state (Fig.~\ref{fig:state}d and Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:heldout}). Good views place 76\% of these records with the right customer. TF-IDF places 53\%, bad views and next-token prediction place 19\% and 20\%, and a guess of the largest customer gets 12\%. They stand in for the 72\% of records without a link, mostly internal chat, which have no ground truth to score.

The same good views also improve an encoder that already knows the language. A frozen pretrained sentence encoder places 53\% of the hidden records with the right customer, about as many as TF-IDF. A small head that we train on good views raises this share to 62\%, and the head trains in about two minutes on a CPU. Thus, the training signal adds to the encoders that companies already use. It does not replace them.

A natural concern is that the space only learns names. When we mask customer names, contacts and identifiers, about half of the customer signal remains on the training records (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}b). With every name and identifier masked, the state space places 33\% of hidden records with the right customer, against 75\% with the names kept. The pretrained encoder places 20\% of the same masked records, and a guess of the largest customer 12\% (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:twochannel}). Wrong links blur the space, but they do not break it (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}a).

The space resolves the customer more sharply than the period. On hidden records, good views find the right customer and quarter 26\% of the time. A reference that knows the customer and picks its busiest quarter gets 26\%. With states by month, good views do better than the same kind of reference, 17\% against 13\% (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:months}). Within one customer, states persist and change gradually. Consecutive quarters have a cosine similarity of 0.88, states six quarters apart 0.38, and different customers in the same quarter 0.05 (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}b).

A company can also share and update the space. One rotation partly aligns two encoders that we train separately, on messages and on systems of record (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:e2}a). We fit it on only 64 pairs of records and test it on other states, whose records both encoders saw in training. A message then finds a record of the same customer and quarter as its top match 20.7\% of the time, against 3.8\% by chance. With bad views or next-token prediction, no rotation helps. Another rotation lets a retrained encoder search the old index, so that stored vectors stay usable across versions of the model (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:e3}).

\section{Access control built into the model}

A learned world model can leak across access boundaries in three ways (Methods). Search can return a record that the reader may not read, and a state can average restricted records into what the reader sees. Training can carry restricted content into the encoder's weights, which then reproduce it for anyone. Filters on search results and states close only the first two paths. We close all three. Each access scope gets its own encoder, trained only on records that the scope may read, and every state comes from those records.

To test the third path, we planted secrets in 150 CRM and call records of five customers (Fig.~\ref{fig:acl}a and Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:acl}). Two readers may not read these records: a support engineer and a software engineer. With an encoder trained on all records, a search for a planted codename returns the right customer's records 100\% of the time. This happens even when we filter the search results and the states. With access control built in, every result is the same with and without the secrets. The support engineer's embeddings are identical whether or not the secrets exist (largest difference 0), a property called non-interference that holds by construction\citep{tiwari2024ifc}.

The guarantee costs accuracy mainly for people who may read little (Fig.~\ref{fig:acl}b and Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:aclrescue}). The account executive and the support engineer may read most records. Their own encoders place held-out records almost as well as an encoder trained on everything: 76\% and 74\%, against 78\% and 75\%. The software engineer and the financial analyst may read 22\% and 9\% of the records, and lose more (28\% against 66\%, 23\% against 65\%). A pretrained backbone holds no company data, so all readers may share it. With a head trained on the analyst's own records, the backbone raises the analyst's placement from 23\% to 43\% without a leak.

For the software engineer, whose records are mostly support tickets, the backbone helps little (28\% and 29\%). Most of the rest of the gap comes from records that a reader may not read at all. No model that respects the access policy can use them (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:acl-results}). Rotations can put the views of different scopes in shared coordinates, but they cannot make a view more accurate (Methods).

\section{The state space indexes a memory}

A memory of a company must find the right records and then read what they say. Training on good views makes a space discard what the views of a state do not share. This includes facts about the records of one system, such as how many tickets a customer opened (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:twochannel}). We therefore give each record two vectors. The state vector says which situation the record belongs to, and the detail vector, from a pretrained encoder, keeps what the record says.

We built a small memory reader in the style of Memorilla\citep{fallahpour2026memorilla}. It reads 16 records that a store finds for each question, and it chooses one of 18 answers (Methods). The questions ask about tickets, priorities, calls and deal stages in one customer's quarter. When a question names its customer, a store of detail vectors answers 71.2\% of the questions (Fig.~\ref{fig:memory}c). No combination of the two channels clearly does better.

Often a question does not name the customer. Instead, it comes with a record, such as the ticket in front of a support engineer. In our test, each question comes with one record of its customer, the anchor, from a hidden thread and another system (Fig.~\ref{fig:memory}a). With the names kept in the records, placement of the anchor with the state channel answers 63.8\% of the questions. Placement with the pretrained encoder answers 54.9\%, and no store answers 52.2\%. Paired over the same questions, the gain over the pretrained encoder is +8.9 points (95\% interval +3.9 to +13.8; per seed +8.7, +11.8, +11.0, +3.9).

The two channels thus do different jobs. The state channel finds the situation, and the detail channel supplies what the records say. A memory should find a question's records through the state space, or by its name, and then read them from the store. Placement with both channels side by side does as well as the state channel alone (64.2\%). With every name masked, neighbours of the anchor by state, read by detail, answer 57.7\%, against 52.0\% without a store. An oracle that knows the anchor's link answers 67.1\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:memory}b).

\section{Similar situations and a first decision layer}

A shared state space also answers the question ``what is this like?'' (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:primitives}). Of the five most similar states of other customers, 13--21\% share a state's situation in every representation, against 8\% by chance. The calendar does not explain this: in other quarters only, the share is 17\% against 7\%.

For customers in trouble, representations that keep detail find other customers in trouble. Bad views find 35\% and the pretrained encoder 25\%, against a base rate of 12\%. The good-view state does not (4\%). Concatenating the two channels does not help here (5\%), so each channel should answer its own kind of query. Invariance is a design choice: the state channel says which situation a record belongs to, and the detail channel says what happens in it.

A world model must also predict how states change, and how decisions change them. We read six kinds of company action from the records, from sales calls to support replies (Methods). We added the actions to a predictor of next month's state, and we built a case-based simulation of decisions for customers in trouble.

On this company's logs, a linear predictor learns mostly that states return toward their mean. Forecasts of next month's facts do not beat the most common answer (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:time}). This month's actions change the predictor's error by $-$0.1\% to +0.6\% of the error without the actions (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:actions}). One reason is that the state already encodes the actions: from the good-view state, a classifier predicts each action with an AUROC of 0.63--0.88.

The decision simulation runs end to end (Fig.~\ref{fig:decisions}). For each of 29 customer-months with an urgent or high-priority ticket during an open deal, it retrieves similar months of other customers. It then splits them by whether the company intervened, and it compares what followed. However, the logs barely contain the alternative: the company intervened in 25 of the 29 months. The similar months with an intervention were themselves in trouble 19--24\% of the time, against 0.2--4\% without one (Fig.~\ref{fig:decisions}a). So the simulation shows where the company acted, not what its actions did.

This result tells what data a decision layer needs. Logs can separate the effect of a decision from the situations that cause it, but only under two conditions\citep{pearl2009causality,hernan2020causal}. They must record what drives each decision, and both decisions must occur in similar situations. Here neither holds. A company learns what its decisions do when it logs them with their reasons, varies some on purpose, or replays them in a simulator. Such a simulator can run one situation with different decisions.

\section{Discussion}

A company already keeps the training signal for the first layer of a model of itself. The links between its systems are good views. Trained on them, small encoders learn one state space for seven systems. This space places records with withheld links, keeps part of its signal without names, and different encoders and versions can share it. Access control is a property of the model. The space serves as the index of a memory and as the base of a decision layer.

A world model of an organization works at three levels: representation, prediction and control (Fig.~\ref{fig:idea}). At the first level, an encoder maps a record $x$ to the state of its situation, $f(x) \approx s$. At the second level, a model maps a state, an action and its context to the next state,
\begin{equation}
\hat{s}_{t+1} = h(s_t, a_t, c_t).
\end{equation}
At the third level, the company chooses the action with the best predicted outcome,
\begin{equation}
a^{*} = \arg\max_{a}\; \mathbb{E}\big[\,U(s_{t+1}) \mid s_t, \mathrm{do}(a)\,\big].
\end{equation}
This paper gives evidence for the first level on records that the model did not see, more for the customer than for the period. It builds a first version of the second level, which so far learns little beyond a return toward the mean. The third level needs decisions whose effects the data can identify.

What does the space learn? By the theorem, it learns what the views of a state share\citep{balestriero2026platonic}. For views of one customer in one quarter, this is mostly the customer, more than the exact period. Finer windows, such as months or weeks, carry more information about time. An objective that predicts the future, which we did not test here, would make the state keep what matters for the future\citep{assran2025vjepa2,zhang2026hjepa}.

The theorem ties the representation to the graph of positive pairs more than to the architecture. So other objectives on the same pairs, such as supervised contrastive learning, should learn a similar space\citep{haochen2021spectral,khosla2020supcon}. The contribution of this work is the training signal that organizations keep, and its tests across systems, versions and access scopes, not one loss.

The learned state does not replace explicit memory. An explicit ledger keeps what happened, who said it, when, and who may see it. The state space keeps which situation each record belongs to and which situations are alike. Our results are consistent with this division. The state channel discards details, such as ticket priorities, that mark a customer in trouble, and the detail channel keeps them. Corporate AI should therefore use the learned state as a view of its memory, not as the memory itself.

Table~\ref{tab:compare} places this work among other ways to give AI systems knowledge of a company. Retrieval, explicit memory and compressed memory organize what the company wrote, so that a model can read it\citep{lewis2020rag,rasmussen2025zep,fallahpour2026memorilla}. Trained approaches compress a corpus into a cache, or teach a model to use notes\citep{eyuboglu2025cartridges,engram2026notes}. Our state space could sit upstream of all of them: it organizes the observations into situations before anything reads them. A Memorilla-style reader could then read situations rather than separate documents, and trained agents could act on a shared state that respects permissions. We tested only the first of these combinations, with a small reader.

Organizations change at several timescales. Tickets move in hours, deals in weeks and strategy in quarters. Hierarchical world models in robotics learn one latent space for each timescale, and they plan from the top down\citep{zhang2026hjepa}. In our recipe, the time window sets the timescale of the state. Thus, a hierarchy of state spaces for weeks, months and quarters is a direct extension.

At this scale, the approach is cheap enough to keep up with a company. Every encoder and head that we timed trains in 2--7 minutes on a CPU, without a GPU. We did not test these costs for a company with millions of records.

A company needs one model for each access scope: a set of people who may read the same records. Under rules by role, a scope is a role. Rules that follow each sender and each direct message, as in our policy, make almost every person a separate scope. Such a company can keep personal records out of training, or share a pretrained backbone across scopes. When an access rule changes, only the models of the scopes that it affects must train again.

The decisive next test is causal. In a simulator with a hidden state and known effects of actions, one customer can face different decisions. Examples are an engineering escalation and a call from an executive. A world model passes the test if it ranks the decisions by their true effects for customers that it did not see. It must also beat a ledger with simple rules, which encodes only the facts and transitions that it records.

Our evidence comes from one simulated company, whose text is cleaner and more regular than real company text. We used small encoders, and most experiments ran with one seed. Larger embedding models may raise the pretrained baselines, and also the results of a good-view head on top of them (Fig.~\ref{fig:state}d). Real deployments add noisy links, ambiguous references and finer access rules. These plug into the same mechanisms, but we did not test them here. Replication on other companies and on real data is the next step (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:limits}).

Every company already has a world model. Its people hold it in their heads, in parts, and the company loses a part each time a person leaves. An explicit model would let people and AI agents understand the company from one state that respects permissions. They could learn on its history of situations and decisions, and test a decision before they make it. We expect corporate AI to run on models of this kind. At Sentra, we work to build them.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{A model of an organization}
We model an organization as a set of entities $e$ (customers, deals, projects, people) whose situation changes over time. We write $s_{e,t}$ for the latent state of entity $e$ during time window $t$. This state is everything that is true about that customer this quarter. It ranges from the open issues and the deal stage to the people involved and the mood of the last call.

The organization never observes states directly. Instead, it observes \emph{records} $x$: an email, a chat message, a ticket comment, a call-transcript turn, a CRM change, an issue, a meeting. A system renders each record from a state, and the record carries nuisance,
\begin{equation}
x = r_m\!\left(s_{e,t},\, n\right),
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the system (email, chat, CRM, \dots) and $n$ collects everything that is not the situation. This nuisance includes the author, the template, the word choice and the format. A world model of the organization has three parts:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item An \textbf{encoder} $f$ that maps any record into a shared state space, $f(x) \approx g(s_{e,t})$, invariant to the system $m$ and the nuisance $n$.
\item A \textbf{memory} that aggregates encoded records into current states and serves them to people and programs.
\item A \textbf{predictor} that maps a state, and possibly an action, to the next state: $s_{e,t+1} \approx h(s_{e,t}, a_t)$.
\end{enumerate}
This work is mostly about the first part, the state space, and about how to keep it stable as the organization changes. It also tests a first version of the second part and the inputs of the third.

\paragraph{Why links are the right training signal.}
Self-supervised learning needs a notion of ``the same''. Suppose that we corrupt a record: we crop it, or we mask or drop words. This says that a record is the same as a damaged copy of itself. The model then learns the word choice and the template of the record, and the system that wrote it. Next-token prediction teaches the model the language of the records. Neither says anything about the company.

Links do: two records linked to the same customer in the same quarter are about the same situation. Different people wrote them, in different systems, for different purposes. What they share is the situation. This is the setting in which the Platonic Representation Theorem predicts convergence.

Links are not annotations: nobody creates them to train a model. They are metadata that the systems keep to do their work. However, they are a form of weak supervision. So we evaluate both on the records used in training and on records whose links we hid.

\subsection{JEPAs and the Platonic Representation Theorem}
\paragraph{JEPAs and LeJEPA.}
To learn an encoder, a joint-embedding predictive architecture makes the embeddings of related views predictable from one another. It does not reconstruct the input\citep{lecun2022path,assran2023ijepa}. LeJEPA\citep{balestriero2025lejepa} replaces the usual heuristics against collapse with SIGReg. SIGReg is a regularizer that pushes the distribution of embeddings toward an isotropic Gaussian. It uses random one-dimensional projections and the Epps--Pulley test. The loss pulls every view's projected embedding toward the mean of the global views and adds SIGReg: $\mathcal{L} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{SIGReg}}$.

\paragraph{The Platonic Representation Theorem.}
\citet{huh2024platonic} conjectured that representations learned by different models and modalities converge as they scale. \citet{balestriero2026platonic} turn the conjecture into a theorem for JEPAs. We use five of its parts. (1) A JEPA trained with SIGReg learns a spectral embedding of the \emph{positive-pair graph}: the graph that says which views count as the same. That graph sets the representation more than the architecture does.

(2) Suppose that the positive pairs of encoders trained on different modalities are two observations of one scene (good views). Then the encoders learn the same representation up to one rotation. Otherwise, the disagreement between the two graphs about which scenes are alike bounds their misalignment. (3) Orthogonal Procrustes\citep{schonemann1966procrustes} identifies that rotation from a small number of anchor pairs.

(4) A JEPA discards nuisance that the views do not share. The fraction of the embedding explained by the scene, $\rho$, measures how much of it is the world. (5) The theorem lets us read next-token prediction as a JEPA with bad views: parts of one document. This explains why language models align with vision only slowly as they scale. The paper validates the theory on images and captions. We test it on an organization.

\subsection{Simulated company and data}
Era by Eon generates synthetic companies whose systems agree with each other. The systems have vendor-compatible interfaces, and each company has exact ground truth\citep{eon2026era,gruenbaum2026era}. We ran seven of its public simulator images: Gmail, Google Calendar, Slack, Salesforce, Zendesk, Gong and Jira. We ran them with one seed, so all seven describe the same fictional company, Northwind Robotics, as of 31~October 2026 (\emph{day~1}). We then advanced the simulators past the close of the next fiscal quarter, 30~January 2027 (\emph{day~2}).

An exporter pulls every record that contains text through each system's API. It puts the records into one table with system, timestamp, author, thread, text and link fields. Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the data.

On day~1, the company has 78{,}048 records. The systems link 21{,}972 of them (28\%) to one of 21 active customers. These linked records belong to 180 customer-quarter states, from 2022~Q4 to 2026~Q4. By day~2, the same customers have 7{,}990 new linked records. Of these, 5{,}961 have dates in the last calendar quarter of 2026 and 2{,}025 in January 2027. The other 4 are calendar events for later months.

Era generates records from one shared company graph. The text comes from templates and from a pre-written text pack. A language model wrote part of this pack. Era fills in the facts of the text from the graph\citep{eon2026era}. Real company text is messier. Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:limits} returns to this point.

\paragraph{Data export.} The seven simulators run as Docker images (\texttt{erabyeon/*}) with seed 42 and high fidelity. We set the simulated date to 31~October 2026. The exporter reads every system through its REST interface. It reads Slack messages, Gmail messages, Google Calendar events and Salesforce objects (accounts, opportunities, tasks, cases and notes). It also reads Zendesk tickets and comments, Gong call transcripts, and Jira issues and comments. Day~2 is the simulators' second state, after the fiscal quarter that closes on 30~January 2027.

We convert the texts to lower case and tokenize them into words. Each record keeps its first 96 tokens. The vocabulary keeps the words that occur at least twice among the linked records. On day~1, the vocabulary has 6{,}061 words. We build it from text only, before any held-out split. All experiments use linked records only.

\subsection{States, links and views}
We use customers as entities and calendar quarters as time windows, so a state is a (customer, quarter) pair. In Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:time}, we also use calendar months and ISO weeks as time windows. A record belongs to a state if its system links it to the customer and its timestamp is in the window.

The links come from the systems themselves. They are a customer's dedicated chat channel, a customer header on an email and the CRM context of a recorded call. They are also the organization of a support ticket, a customer field on an issue and the account of a CRM object. For calendar events, the link is the call that they schedule.

The encoder never reads these links. The links only decide which records are positives. In our experiments, we keep only linked records, so that we can score every record against the ground truth. To see how the model places records without links, we hide the links of a quarter of the threads during training (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:heldout}).

\paragraph{Views.}
Each batch has 256 anchor records, and we draw them uniformly. Thus each state gets a weight in proportion to its number of records. Each anchor gets two global views and six local views, as in LeJEPA's multi-crop recipe.

With \good, each view is a record that we draw at random, with replacement, from the anchor's state. Thus the views are usually \emph{different records}, from any system. We trim each view to a contiguous span, and we mask 15\% of the tokens. The span keeps 50--100\% of the tokens of the record for a global view and 15--50\% for a local view.

With \textcolor{badorange}{\textbf{bad views}}, all eight views are copies of the anchor record itself, and we trim and mask each copy. This is standard corruption-based self-supervision for text. Everything else is identical, so the only difference is the definition of ``the same''.

\subsection{Encoders and training}
We want the encoder to learn from the company's data alone, with no pretrained knowledge of language. Thus we use a deliberately small model, and we train it from scratch. Unigrams and hashed bigrams ($2^{18}$ buckets) feed a 192-dimensional embedding bag. A two-layer MLP follows the bag. Its 256-dimensional output is the representation that we evaluate. We use a projector (1{,}024 hidden units, 128 outputs) only for the loss.

We train with LeJEPA's loss ($\lambda = 0.05$, 256 random slices) for 1{,}500 steps with batches of 256 anchor records. The peak learning rate is $10^{-3}$, with 5\% warm-up and cosine decay. We use sparse Adam for the embedding table and AdamW for all other parameters. One run takes three to six minutes on a CPU.

The \textcolor{ntpaqua}{\textbf{next-token}} baseline trains the same encoder on random prefixes of records. Its softmax head covers the whole vocabulary of 6{,}061 words.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.} The encoder is an embedding bag of $2^{18}$ buckets $\times$ 192, followed by LayerNorm, Linear(192, 512), GELU and Linear(512, 256). The projector is Linear(256, 1024), followed by BatchNorm, GELU and Linear(1024, 128). The loss is $(1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{SIGReg}}$, with $\lambda = 0.05$. SIGReg uses 256 random directions and 17 integration knots on $[-5,5]$. We use 2 global views (50--100\% contiguous span) and 6 local views (15--50\%), with 15\% token masking.

We train for 1{,}500 steps (1{,}000 for the per-family encoders) with batch size 256. The peak learning rate is $10^{-3}$, with a 5\% linear warm-up and cosine decay. We use AdamW (weight decay 0.05) for the dense parameters and sparse Adam for the embedding bag. The next-token baseline uses 1{,}024 random prefixes per batch and a softmax over the whole vocabulary.

\subsection{A pretrained backbone with a good-view head}
To test whether the recipe also improves the kind of encoder used in practice, we freeze a small pretrained sentence encoder. This encoder is all-MiniLM-L6-v2\citep{reimers2019sbert,wang2020minilm}, with 384 dimensions. It learned from public text and never from this company's records. We train only a head on its outputs, with the same good views, projector and loss, for 800 steps. The head is LayerNorm, Linear(384, 512), GELU and Linear(512, 256).

Because we freeze the backbone, we embed each record once. A view is a record that we draw from the anchor's state. We do not trim or mask the views. Instead, each view gets 10\% feature dropout and Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.02$). The head takes about two minutes to train on a CPU.

\paragraph{Pretrained backbone.} The backbone is all-MiniLM-L6-v2 with mean pooling and normalized outputs. We apply it to the raw text of each record (at most 256 word pieces). The head is LayerNorm(384), followed by Linear(384, 512), GELU and Linear(512, 256). The projector and the loss are as above ($\lambda = 0.05$). We train for 800 steps with batch size 256.

Each anchor has eight views, two global and six local. Here, we construct the global and local views in the same way, because we cannot crop a frozen embedding. Each view is a record drawn with replacement from the anchor's state. We apply 10\% feature dropout and Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.02 to each view. We use AdamW with a peak learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and cosine decay. For the held-out test, we train the head on the training threads only.

\subsection{Baselines and metrics}
Bad views and next-token prediction isolate the effect of the views. They use the same text, the same encoder and the same budget. TF-IDF with latent semantic analysis (256 dimensions) is a classical text representation. A randomly initialized encoder is a floor. We also use the frozen pretrained encoder, on its own and with a good-view head. This comparison tests whether the recipe transfers to an encoder that already knows the language.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
\emph{Cross-system precision@10}: we take the ten nearest neighbours of a record by cosine similarity. We search for these neighbours only among linked records from \emph{other systems} and other threads. The metric is the fraction of these neighbours that concern the same customer, or the same customer and quarter. We average it over 3{,}000 linked records. The restriction to other systems makes this a test of a shared state space rather than of a shared template.

\emph{Placement} (held-out records): we assign each record to the training state whose mean embedding is nearest. We report the share assigned to the right customer and the share assigned to the right customer and quarter.

\emph{Cross-family probes}: we train a linear classifier to predict the customer from message embeddings. We test it on the embeddings of records from the systems of record. The reverse probe trains on the systems of record and tests on messages.

\emph{$\rho$ and $R^2$}: these give the fraction of the embedding that the state, the customer or the system explains. We compute them on the top 32 dimensions, which we whiten. We compute the fraction from the between-group covariance, with the within-group bias removed. $\rho$ is the theorem's scene-explained fraction.

\emph{Alignment}: we measure same-state recall@1 when messages retrieve records after the rotation, on held-out states. Before the rotation, we measure mutual ten-nearest-neighbour agreement.

\emph{Time}: we use customer probes on new-quarter records and the per-direction correlation between old and rotated new embeddings. We also use the precision@10 of new queries against the old index. We define the measures of drift, misalignment and similar situations where we use them (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:primitives}).

\paragraph{Evaluation details.} Cross-system precision@10 excludes neighbours from the query's own system and thread. It averages over 3{,}000 random linked records. Probes are multinomial logistic regressions. $\rho$ and $R^2$ use the top 32 dimensions after we whiten the embeddings. We correct the between-group covariance for its finite-sample bias.

For the alignment test, we split the 126 states observed by both families in half. The anchors come from one half, and the 1{,}000 test pairs come from the other. The per-direction analysis of the retrained encoder works in the centred (not whitened) space. It fits one rotation and one global scale on 2{,}000 day-1 records embedded by both models. It tests the fit on 6{,}000 other records. Its variance-weighted $R^2$ is 73\% (88\% on the top 8 directions).

The second retraining test in Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:e3} is a separate run. It uses a retrained encoder with a different seed and no hidden records. It fits a rotation without scale on 256 anchors, in a whitened 64-dimensional space.

For the held-out test, we draw threads at random among the linked records. We fit TF-IDF on the training records only. For the state probes and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}b, we refit TF-IDF on the day-2 corpus.

\subsection{Masking and wrong links}
\paragraph{Masking.} Before tokenization, we replace customer account and organization names, and the distinctive words in them, with one placeholder token. We keep words that several customer names share, such as ``payments''. We also replace customer web domains, contact names, email addresses, identifiers of records, cases and requests, and long numbers. The second mask also replaces the company's own employees' names, email handles, and chat real and display names. In an employee's name, it replaces every word of three letters or more. The second mask changes 86\% of the linked records.

For the test of wrong links, we attached a share of the linked records to a random wrong state before training. We then evaluated against the true states (one seed; Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:noise}).

\subsection{Alignment across systems and versions}
To test whether separately trained models agree, we split the systems into two families. The first family is the messages that people write to each other (email and chat). The second family is the systems of record (CRM, support, calls, calendar and issues). We train one encoder per family. Each encoder sees only the records of its own family and never a cross-family pair.

We whiten the output of each encoder to 64 dimensions without labels. We then fit one orthogonal map between the two spaces by Procrustes on $k$ anchor pairs. Each anchor pair is a message and a record that belong to the same state.

\paragraph{Stability over time: a rotation registry.}
When new data arrives, we retrain the encoder. We do not re-embed everything in the store. Instead, we fit one rotation and one global scale from the new model's space to the old one. We fit them on records that both models embedded. We then rotate new queries into the old space. A registry of such rotations keeps every stored vector usable across model versions.

\subsection{Access control}
\label{sec:acl}
Every record carries its \emph{readers}, the people allowed to read it. The system that wrote the record already defines them. For a private channel, they are its members, and for an email thread, they are its participants. For a ticket or a CRM record, they are the teams that can open it. We write $D_u$ for the records that reader $u$ may read.

A world model leaks across access boundaries in three ways. Each way needs its own control:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Search} can return a record that $u$ may not read. Control: search only $D_u$, the usual fix in retrieval systems.
\item \textbf{Aggregation} can mix restricted records into what $u$ sees. A customer's state vector is an average over its records. One private escalation thread shifts this average. Control: compute every state that $u$ sees from $D_u$ only. Because states are averages, this costs one masked mean per state at read time.
\item \textbf{Training} can carry restricted content into the weights. An encoder trained on every record learns associations. One such association connects a codename used only in leadership discussions to the customer that it refers to. The encoder then reproduces these associations for anyone who queries it. Control: serve $u$ an encoder trained only on records in $D_u$, with a vocabulary built from them.
\end{enumerate}
With all three controls, every output that $u$ receives is a function of $D_u$ alone. These outputs are search results, states and embeddings of $u$'s own queries. When someone changes, adds or deletes any record outside $D_u$, nothing that $u$ receives changes. This property is \emph{non-interference}\citep{tiwari2024ifc}. Here it holds by construction, not by estimate.

In practice, each access scope gets its own encoder. An access scope is a team, a role or a group of people who read the same records. The result is one world model with many views. Each view is complete within its reader's rights and empty outside them.

\paragraph{What readers may share.}
Per-reader encoders cost accuracy for people who may read little (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:acl-results}). Non-interference allows a reader's model to depend on $D_u$ and also on anything that contains no record outside $D_u$. Everyone can share two such things. The first is knowledge that contains no company data at all, such as a sentence encoder pretrained on public text. The second is the records that \emph{every} reader may read.

We test both. For the first, we use a frozen pretrained backbone with a head trained per reader on $D_u$. For the second, we train a base on the records that everyone may read, and we fine-tune it per reader.

Rotations between the spaces of different scopes can place different people's views in shared coordinates. This is possible because encoders trained on different sets of records partly agree up to a rotation (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:e2}).

However, rotations respect non-interference in only two cases. In the first case, the person who receives the result may read the target space, such as the public base. In the second case, this person has rights to both scopes. The reason is that we fit a rotation toward another scope's space with that scope's encoder. Thus the rotation depends on records outside $D_u$.

A rotation also cannot make a view more accurate. It changes the coordinates of a reader's vectors and keeps every distance between them. Thus a reader's search and placement are exactly as accurate after the rotation as before it. A rotation could help only if it brought in another scope's states or index, and non-interference forbids this. Rotations make views comparable, but only information that the reader has a right to can make a view more accurate.

\paragraph{Policy and leak test.}
The role-based policy of Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:policy} (\texttt{cwm/acl.py}) derives the readers of each record from its system, channel and sender. The second part of that table gives the share of customer-linked records that each evaluated reader may read, overall and by system.

For the leak tests, we used 150 training records of five customers in Salesforce and Gong. We appended a unique codename and the sentence ``Privately we expect them to churn to a competitor next quarter.'' to these records. Neither the support engineer nor the software engineer may read them.

We train each reader's encoder with the same recipe as the main comparison. We use good views and one seed, and we hold out a quarter of the threads. We train the encoder on the records that the reader may read, with a vocabulary built from these records.

\paragraph{Access control with a backbone.} The backbone embeds the canary records with their planted text. We train each reader's head on that reader's readable training records only (800 steps). The public base is a head trained for 800 steps on the 534 customer-linked records that all twelve employees may read. This training leaves out the held-out threads. We then fine-tune the head for 800 steps on the reader's records.

For the scope test, we sample with a fixed seed as many records from all systems as the reader may read. We train the scratch encoder on this sample, with the held-out threads hidden and a vocabulary built from the sample. Then we place the reader's held-out readable records against the reader's readable training states.

\subsection{Two channels}
Training on good views teaches a representation to discard what the views of a state do not share. This includes which system wrote a record. That is what makes the space shared. The same training also discards facts that are about one system's records, such as how many tickets a customer opened (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:twochannel}).

We therefore give each record two vectors. The \emph{state} vector comes from good views and says which situation the record belongs to. The \emph{detail} vector comes from a representation that we do not train to ignore which system wrote the record. For this representation, we use bad views or the pretrained encoder. The detail vector keeps what the record specifically says.

We combine the two vectors in two ways. \emph{Concatenation} normalizes each channel and puts the two channels side by side. Methods gives the normalization that we use in each experiment. \emph{Routing} uses the state channel to decide which records are relevant and the detail channel to read them.

\paragraph{Two channels.} For the state probes, we standardize the state matrix of each channel per dimension. We then divide it by the square root of its dimension and concatenate the channels. This way, both channels carry the same total variance.

For the operational primitives and for a reader's own two channels, we put the two L2-normalized embeddings side by side. In the memory store, the concatenated rows put the L2-normalized good-view and pretrained embeddings side by side before the store's PCA map. The routed store keeps the pretrained rows as content and the good-view rows, with the same time code, as retrieval keys. It embeds questions with both encoders.

\subsection{Memory reader and questions}
We store each linked record once, as a row. We map the record's embedding to 112 dimensions with PCA and whiten it. The row holds this embedding, a 16-dimensional sinusoidal time code and a one-hot code of the record's system.

The store's encoder embeds each question. A Memorilla-style module\citep{fallahpour2026memorilla} turns this embedding into 16 query-conditioned slots. The slots attend, through two Perceiver-style cross-attention blocks, either over the whole store or over 16 retrieved rows. The retrieved rows are the rows of the question's quarter whose content in the retrieval channel is most similar to the question's.

An earlier version of this paper ranked whole rows, in which the time code outweighed the content. In that version, only 24\%--35\% of the rows retrieved belonged to the asked customer. When we rank content within the quarter, this share rises to 65\%--71\%. With this change, the conclusions of Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:twochannel} stay the same. The rows of Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:memory} for the reader over the whole store come from that earlier version. The reader in these rows does not retrieve.

We average the slots and match the average against fixed embeddings of the 18 possible answers. These embeddings come from a pretrained sentence encoder (all-MiniLM-L6-v2). Thus, the reader is a classifier with a frozen output layer rather than a language model.

We generate questions from Era's ground truth for each customer-quarter. They ask about the number of support tickets, the worst ticket priority, whether a sales call happened and the deal stage. We split the customer-quarters 70/10/20 into training, validation and test. This gives 36 test customer-quarters and 144 test questions.

We first pre-train the module for 250 steps to recover what the store holds about a customer and date. This includes the share of records from each system and their volume. We then train it on the questions for 12 epochs. We keep the checkpoint that does best on validation.

With the pretrained store, we also embed the questions with the pretrained encoder. This changes the closed-book baseline. Runs are deterministic for a given seed and thread count, but not across thread counts.

\subsection{Questions that come with a record}
\paragraph{Anchored questions.} We use the questions above, but we replace the customer's name with ``this customer''. We anchor each question with one record of its customer-quarter, drawn at random with a different draw for each seed. The record comes from a held-out thread (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:heldout}) and from a system other than the one that answers the question. Zendesk answers questions about tickets and priority, Gong answers questions about calls, and Salesforce answers questions about the deal stage. We drop the questions without such a record. This leaves 127 of the 144 test questions and 393 training questions.

With names masked, we use the second mask of Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:mask} on the stored records. It masks every customer name, employee name and identifier. With names kept, we do not change the records. The state channel is a good-view encoder trained on the stored records, with the held-out threads hidden (one run). The detail channel is all-MiniLM-L6-v2, applied to the same text.

Every stored row has a content vector and a retrieval key. The content vector is the state, detail or concatenated embedding mapped by PCA, a time code and a one-hot code of the system. For the provenance-only reader, the content vector is the time and system codes alone. Every key ranks the records of the question's quarter first.

\emph{Neighbours}: the cosine similarity of the finding channel's content alone sets the rest of the order. The query is the anchor's own content. The anchor itself is among the records found. With names masked, the anchor's own thread supplies 19\% of these records on average.

\emph{Placement}: we key each linked training record by its customer-quarter. We key each held-out record, and the anchor, by the customer whose centroid of linked training records in the finding channel is nearest. The quarter in the key is the record's quarter for stored records and the question's quarter for the anchor. \emph{Link oracle}: we key every record, the anchor included, by its true customer-quarter.

Placement and oracle keys get small random noise, fixed for each seed. We use this noise so that the reader sees 16 random records of the key's customer-quarter. The reader sees the same records for every question about that customer-quarter. We fill each customer-quarter that has fewer than 16 records with records of other states. This is why the oracle's records are not all from the right customer.

We condition the reader's slots on the question text. The question text gives the kind of fact and the quarter, but not the customer. Pre-training uses the same construction. In pre-training, the anchor is a random record of a training thread. The question is ``What happened with this customer?'' We build the targets from the links of training threads only.

\subsection{Time windows and dynamics}
\paragraph{Months.} Month states are (customer, calendar month) pairs. Week states use ISO weeks. The month encoder uses the recipe of the main comparison, with month states as positives. We train it on the training threads of the held-out split.

Next-month probes use states from the day-2 corpus, embedded by the day-1 encoders. We standardize these states and reduce them to 32 principal components. We fit the probes by logistic regression and hold out one customer at a time. A transition links a month to the next calendar month of the same customer when both months have records.

We fit the dynamics models per held-out customer on the other customers' transitions. The models work in the first 32 principal components of the standardized states. One model is a ridge regression from a state to its change. It uses $\alpha = 10$, or an $\alpha \in \{0.1, 1, 10, 10^2, 10^3, 10^4\}$ that we choose by leave-one-customer-out validation within the training customers. The other model is a single factor $k$ that minimizes $\lVert s_{t+1} - k\, s_t \rVert^2$.

For each customer-quarter, the state vector is the mean of the L2-normalized embeddings of its records. These embeddings come from the frozen day-1 encoders, applied to all records through day~2. For the probes, we standardize the state vectors and reduce them to 32 principal components. We fit the probes by logistic regression and hold out one customer at a time. This way, no probe can memorize a customer.

``Now'' predicts a quarter's facts from its own state. ``Next'' predicts the following quarter's facts from the current state (165 transitions). The majority class is the share of the most common answer. Oracle persistence copies the current quarter's true facts. Thus, it reads the ground truth directly.

\subsection{Similar situations, drift and misalignment}
\paragraph{Primitives.} Events come from Era's ground truth. The events are urgent or high-priority tickets (by creation date) and changes of an opportunity's stage (by the date of the change). Drift uses weekly states with at least three records. We score a week if the customer has at least one weekly state in the previous eight weeks. We score it against the mean of up to four of these states. The volume score is the absolute log ratio between the week's number of records and the mean of the same previous weeks.

Misalignment uses customer-months with at least two records from Salesforce or Gong and at least two from Zendesk or Jira. A month is at risk if the customer's worst ticket that month is urgent or high and its latest opportunity is in an open stage. The open stages are prospecting, qualification, proposal and negotiation.

Similar situations use customer-quarter states with at least five records. We centre these states and compare them by cosine similarity. A quarter's situation is its ticket bucket and its deal-stage group. The ticket buckets are none, one or two, three to five, and six or more. The deal-stage groups are open, closed won, closed lost, and no open deal. Chance is the average share of other customers' states that share the query's situation.

\subsection{Actions, outcomes and decisions}
\paragraph{Actions and outcomes.} We define the actions per customer and calendar month of the day-2 corpus. A \emph{sales call} is a call in Era's ground truth that started in the month. A \emph{leader} action is a linked email or chat message that the CEO or the head of engineering wrote. We match authors to employees by email address or chat id. A \emph{CRM task} is a Salesforce task record. An \emph{engineering escalation} is a Jira issue record linked to the customer.

A \emph{support reply} is a Zendesk comment written by an agent. An agent is an author who comments on the tickets of at least three customers. There are four such authors. An \emph{outbound email} is an email on the customer's threads written by an employee. Each action is a 0/1 indicator. The \emph{system mix} of a month is the share of the customer's linked records in it that each of the seven systems wrote.

The outcome \emph{deal progress} is an opportunity of the customer that moves to a later stage or to closed won in the month. The outcome \emph{escalation} is an urgent or high-priority ticket created in the month. A customer-month is \emph{in trouble} when it has an escalation and one of the customer's opportunities is in an open stage. This definition uses the opportunity stages at the end of the month. In Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:primitives}, we use the stage of the customer's latest opportunity.

The 396 transitions are those between consecutive months of the 21 customers. The 398 transitions of Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:time} also include prospects.

The action-conditioned predictor works in the first 32 principal components of the month states. We standardize the month states over all months (an unsupervised step). We fit the components without the held-out customer. The predictor standardizes the indicators and scales them to the spread of the state components. It chooses the ridge strength from $\{1, 10, 10^2, 10^3, 10^4\}$ by leave-one-customer-out validation within the training customers.

Outcome models, and the models that predict each action from the state, are logistic regressions ($C = 0.5$). They use the first 16 components of each representation's month state. We fit them without the held-out customer and hold out one customer at a time. Intervals are percentiles of 2{,}000 bootstrap resamples of customers, with the predictions held fixed.

\paragraph{Event windows and decision simulation.} Event windows contain the customer's linked records in the 14 days before and after the action. They contain no record of the action's kind. For sales calls, the records of this kind are all call transcripts and calendar events. For engineering escalations, they are all Jira issues. For leaders, they are all records written by a leader. The windows need at least two records on each side.

A leader event is a leader's message after 14 days without one. We pair each event with a time drawn from the same customer's records, when we find one in 20 random draws. This time has no action of that kind within 14 days and at least two records on each side. For leaders, no action of that kind means no leader message. Of the 66 leader events, 29 found such a time, and every sales call and engineering escalation did.

The state of a window is the mean of its records' good-view embeddings. The shift is 1 $-$ cosine between the two windows. We regress the shift, by least squares, on the event indicator and the logarithms of the two windows' record counts. The adjusted difference is the coefficient of the event indicator in this fit. Intervals are percentiles of 2{,}000 bootstrap resamples of customers.

To predict the window after from the window before, we use the ridge regression above, in 32 principal components. We use it with and without a one-hot indicator of the action. We hold out one customer at a time.

Decision simulation standardizes each representation's month states and reduces them to 16 principal components. We fit these components once, without labels, on the 396 months that have a following month. The simulation compares the reduced states by cosine.

For each month in trouble, we take the 60 most similar months of the other customers. They can be any month with a following month. We split them by whether the company intervened that month. The first 15 months of each group give the outcome rates.

The outcome model is a logistic regression ($C = 0.5$) on the good-view components and an intervention indicator. We fit it on the other customers' months and evaluate it with the indicator set to 1 and to 0. Intervals are percentiles of 300 bootstrap resamples of customers. In each resample, we repeat the neighbour search and refit the outcome model.

Balance is the share of each group's months that were themselves in trouble, or had an urgent ticket. The propensity AUROC is that of a logistic regression that predicts intervention from the 16 components. This regression holds out one customer at a time.

The day-2 export ends in January 2027. One of the 396 transitions leads from January into February 2027. That next month holds only a planned calendar event, so its outcomes are zero by construction. Its January month is also one of the 29 months in trouble, and the company intervened in that month. Without it, the observed rates in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:simulate} are 46\% and 25\% for deals, and 71\% and 75\% for urgent tickets.

\section{Supplementary Information}

\suppnote{The main comparison on the training records}{sec:e1}
Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:e1} and Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:e1} measure how each objective organizes its own training records. With good views, 95.1\% of a record's ten nearest neighbours from other systems concern the same customer. With the same text and model, this share is 13.6\% for bad views and 13.2\% for next-token prediction. TF-IDF reaches 34.6\%, and a randomly initialized encoder reaches 13.2\%. Thus, with this small encoder, corruption-based views and next-token prediction learn almost nothing about customers.

For the state (customer and quarter together), good views reach 39.0\%, against about 4--5\% for the other two objectives. A model that recognized only the customer could reach at most 16.3\%. Thus the good-view space also separates a customer's quarters. A linear probe trained on messages names the customer of a CRM, support or call record 88\% of the time with good views. With the other objectives, it names the customer about a quarter of the time.

The theorem's scene-explained fraction $\rho$ is about 0.7 for good views. It is below 0.2 for every other representation trained from scratch. The system that wrote a record explains about 4\% of the good-view embedding.

Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}a--c shows the difference. With good views, each customer's records from every system gather in a few tight regions. With the other objectives, they scatter among the records of all other customers.

The small encoder trained from scratch gives a clean comparison, but it is not what anyone would deploy. With it, bad views and next-token prediction do no better than a random encoder. The last two rows of Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:e1} repeat the measurement with a pretrained sentence encoder. With the pretrained encoder on its own, 40\% of a record's cross-system neighbours concern the same customer, more than with TF-IDF.

Training only a small head on its frozen outputs with good views raises this share to 72\%. This training also more than doubles the cross-family probe from messages to records, and it roughly doubles $\rho$. The recipe therefore also improves an encoder that already knows the language. On this company, the pretrained encoder with the head does not reach the encoder trained from scratch. The encoder trained from scratch can learn the company's own vocabulary, such as product names, error codes and people. A frozen general-purpose encoder may compress such details away.

We measure these numbers on the records used for training, with the same customer-quarter groups that defined the positive pairs. These numbers show how well each objective absorbs the links, not how well it generalizes. The next experiment tests generalization.

\suppnote{Records with hidden links}{sec:heldout}
We hid a quarter of the threads from training. These threads contain 5{,}366 records whose links the model never saw. We trained each objective on the rest. We then placed each hidden record at the training state whose mean embedding is nearest (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:heldout}).

Good views place 76\% of the hidden records with the right customer and 26\% with the right customer and quarter. TF-IDF places 53\% and 14\%, and bad views place 19\% and 4\%. Next-token prediction places 20\% and 4\%. On its own, the pretrained encoder places 53\% with the right customer, about as many as TF-IDF. With a good-view head trained on the same training threads, it places 62\%.

If we knew each record's customer and chose that customer's busiest quarter, we would place 26\% with the right state. This is about as many as good views place. So on records that the model did not see, the gain is in the identification of the customer. It is not in the separation of that customer's quarters. Monthly windows change this a little (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:time}).

Among the hidden records themselves, 70\% of a record's ten nearest neighbours from other systems concern the same customer with good views. With TF-IDF, this share is 26\%.

Records that carry no link at all are 72\% of this company's records. They are in a similar position, although they may differ in kind from linked records. Most of them are chat messages. We cannot score them directly because they have no ground truth.

\suppnote{Masked names and wrong links}{sec:mask}
\label{sec:noise}
A natural worry is that the encoder merely learns to spot names. This happens elsewhere: embeddings of earnings calls mostly encode company identity\citep{yu2024samecompany}. In every record, we masked the customers' names and their distinctive words, contact names, email addresses, web domains and record identifiers. We then retrained the good-view encoder on the masked records (one seed). On the training records, same-customer precision across systems falls from 95\% to 48\%. TF-IDF falls from 35\% to 14\% (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}b).

However, the company's own employees could still reveal the customer of a record. An account executive or support engineer who handles a fixed set of accounts identifies the customer as well as its name does. We therefore also masked the employees' names, email handles and chat names, in addition to everything above, and retrained again. This mask changes 18{,}936 linked records, against 18{,}832 for the customer mask alone. The customer mask already changed most records that name an employee.

Same-customer precision is 47\% (TF-IDF: 14\%). State-level precision is 25\%, still above the 16.3\% that a model would get if it knew only the customer. Employee names therefore carried almost none of the signal that remained. Roughly half of the customer signal is names and identifiers. The other half comes from what the records say.

Real links are imperfect. People file some tickets under the wrong organization, and some duplicate customer records stay unmerged. The theorem bounds the damage by how much the wrong links change the positive-pair graph.

We attached 25\% and 50\% of linked records to a random wrong state before training (one seed). We then evaluated against the true states (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}a). With 25\% of the links wrong, same-customer precision is 84\%, against 95\% with clean links. State precision is 32\%, against 39\%. With 50\% of the links wrong, the two values are 68\% and 22\%. Even then, same-customer precision is far above that of bad views and next-token prediction with clean links (14\% and 13\%). Wrong links blur the space, but they do not break it.

\suppnote{Alignment, new versions and persistence}{sec:e2}
\label{sec:e3}
We trained one encoder on messages and another on systems of record. No pair crossed the two. We joined the two encoders with one orthogonal map (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:e2}a). We fitted a rotation on 64 anchor pairs and measured retrieval on states held out from the fit. A message then retrieves a record of the same customer and quarter as its top match 20.7\% of the time, against 3.8\% by chance. With 2{,}000 anchors, the share is 25.1\%.

Same-customer recall@1 is 41--50\% against 14\% by chance. With bad views or next-token prediction, no rotation helps. Same-state recall stays at 4--6\%, near chance. Before any rotation, the mutual ten-nearest-neighbour agreement between the two spaces is 0.15 with good views and 0.02 with either alternative.

The alignment is partial. A message finds a record of the same customer and quarter as its top match only about a fifth of the time. Messages and systems of record observe different aspects of a customer's situation, but the theorem's guarantee assumes that they observe the same state. Also, the two encoders share a vocabulary, and we trained them on the records of the test states. We held out these states only from the rotation fit. Still, with good views a single rotation finds shared structure, and with the alternatives it finds none, consistent with the theorem's qualitative prediction.

\paragraph{New records.} We trained a customer probe on day-1 embeddings. We tested it on the 7{,}990 records written after training. With good views, the probe assigns 72.6\% of these records to the right customer, against 41.0\% for next-token prediction and 30.5\% for bad views. The day-1 encoders never saw these records.

\paragraph{New models.} We retrained the good-view encoder on all data through day~2. We hid the threads of 2{,}004 new records from training. Then we fitted one rotation and one global scale from the new space to the old. The fit used 2{,}000 day-1 records that both models embedded.

Along the ten leading principal directions of the old space, the old and rotated new embeddings correlate at 0.91--0.97. From the 33rd direction on, the mean correlation is 0.19 (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:e3}a). The leading directions carry most of the variance. The first 16 directions carry 85\%. As a result, the rotation explains 73\% of the variance, but it does not explain the low-variance tail.

We then used hidden new records as queries against the old index. Neither model saw these records in training. When the retrained model embeds these records and we rotate the embeddings, they retrieve the same customer with 79.4\% precision@10. The precision is 2.9\% without the rotation and 71.6\% when the old model embeds the records (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:e3}b).

Not every measure favours good views. In a second test, we retrained a good-view encoder and a bad-view encoder. We scored this test in a whitened 64-dimensional space that weights every direction equally. A rotation fitted on 256 anchors explains less of the retrained good-view encoder than of the retrained bad-view encoder. The $R^2$ is $-0.22$ against $0.28$, and the mutual ten-nearest-neighbour agreement is 0.36 against 0.59.

One plausible explanation is that bad-view encoders mostly encode surface features of the text, which any retrained encoder reproduces. By the same explanation, the good-view encoder's leading directions, which carry the state, are stable, but its tail is not. A rotation registry therefore preserves the part of the space that search relies on, not the whole space.

\paragraph{States persist and drift.} We averaged the records of each customer-quarter into a state vector and compared the states (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}b). For this, we used frozen day-1 encoders and all records through day~2. With good views, a customer's states in consecutive quarters have a cosine similarity of 0.88. This similarity falls smoothly to 0.38 for states six quarters apart. Different customers in the same quarter have a similarity of 0.05.

With bad views and next-token prediction, consecutive quarters are only 0.55--0.57 alike. Different customers are 0.22--0.25 alike. In the good-view space, a customer's state is persistent but changes gradually. Whether those changes are predictable is the question of Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:time}.

\suppnote{Access control}{sec:acl-results}
\paragraph{Who may read what.} We gave every record its readers under a role-based policy for the simulated company (Methods). The CEO may read 100\% of the customer-linked records. The support engineer may read 86\% of them, and the account executive may read 81\%. The software engineer may read 22\%, and the financial analyst may read 9\%.

\paragraph{Leaks.} We planted secrets in 150 CRM and call records of five customers. Neither the support engineer nor the software engineer may read these records. The secrets are a unique codename per customer and one sentence that is the same for all five. This sentence says that the company expects the customer to churn to a competitor.

We then measured what each of the two readers could find. We compared every configuration with the same configuration built without the secrets (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:acl}, Fig.~\ref{fig:acl}a). The \emph{access advantage} is the share found with the secrets minus the share found without them. Without access control, the secrets are plainly visible. A codename search returns the right customer's records 100\% of the time.

The usual fix is to filter search results and states to readable records. This fix does not stop the leak. The encoder learned the codenames and the sentence from the restricted records. It places them next to the right customers' \emph{readable} records.

A codename search still returns the right customer's records 100\% of the time for the support engineer and 100\% for the software engineer. Without the secrets, the shares are 0\% and 0\%. The secret sentence puts 80\% and 60\% of the five canary customers among the five most similar customers. Without the secrets, the shares are 40\% and 20\%.

With access control built in, the readers' encoders never saw the restricted records. The codenames are not even in their vocabularies. Every result is the same with and without the secrets.

\paragraph{Non-interference, checked.} We trained the support engineer's encoder twice, on the corpus with the planted secrets and on the corpus without them. Its training data was byte-for-byte identical. Every embedding that it produced was also identical (largest difference: 0). The encoder trained on everything changed.

\paragraph{Cost.} What a reader gives up depends on how much they may read (Fig.~\ref{fig:acl}b). The CEO reads everything, so nothing changes. The account executive and the support engineer may read most records, and they lose little. Their own encoders place held-out readable records with the right customer 76\% and 74\% of the time. For the encoder trained on everything, the shares are 78\% and 75\%.

The software engineer and the financial analyst may read 22\% and 9\% of the records, and they lose much more. The shares are 28\% against 66\% for the software engineer, and 23\% against 65\% for the financial analyst. Their encoders learn from few records, mostly from one or two systems.

\paragraph{Recovery of accuracy within the guarantee.} The encoder trained on everything is not a fair target. Its better placement of a reader's records comes partly from records that the reader may not read. Non-interference forbids this. We tested what a reader can legitimately recover with the two kinds of shared knowledge of Methods (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:aclrescue}).

With a frozen pretrained backbone and a good-view head trained on each reader's records, the financial analyst's placement rises from 23\% to 43\%. Even the backbone with no head places 35\%. The software engineer barely gains. The placement is 28\% with their own encoder, 29\% with the backbone and a head, and 32\% with both side by side.

For readers who may read most records, the backbone is worse than their own encoder. The CEO gets 62\% with the backbone and a head, against 76\% with their own encoder. A frozen general-purpose encoder with a head is weaker on this company than a small encoder trained on its records. As a result, the backbone can help only where a reader has little data of their own. It does not always help there.

The backbone does not weaken the guarantee. With heads trained on readable records, the access advantage for codenames and for the secret sentence is +0.00 and +0.00 for the support engineer. For the software engineer, it is +0.00 and +0.00. Each reader's embeddings are identical with and without the secrets (largest difference 0 and 0).

A head trained on every record also leaks through filtered search. For the two readers, the codename advantage is +0.28 and +0.40. With the scratch encoder, it is +1.00 and +1.00. The secret-sentence advantage is +0.60 and +0.60. With the scratch encoder, it is +0.40 and +0.40.

The financial analyst may read the CRM records that carry the planted secrets. The leak test therefore does not apply to that reader. Their guarantee, like everyone's, rests on construction.

The second kind of shared knowledge does not help here. Only 534 customer-linked records are readable by every employee (public chat channels and issues). A head trained on them alone places 17\% of the software engineer's held-out records and 38\% of the financial analyst's. We trained a head on them first and then fine-tuned it on each reader's records for as many steps as a head trained directly. This head places 25\% and 41\%, against 29\% and 43\% for the head trained directly on the reader's records.

We did not run rotations as an experiment, because geometry fixes their effect on accuracy. Placement by the nearest state centroid under cosine similarity does not change under any rotation of the reader's space. A unit test in the repository checks this.

We now ask why the two readers differ and where the loss comes from. Almost all of what the software engineer may read (87\%) is support tickets. The backbone alone places this reader's records poorly: 15\%, against 35\% for the financial analyst's records. The financial analyst's records are mostly CRM records. Support tickets in this company share templates across customers. A general-purpose encoder finds little in them that identifies the customer.

To separate the number of records a reader may read from their kind, we trained the same scratch encoder on a random sample. This sample had records from all systems, as many as each reader may read (4{,}733 for the software engineer). We then used the encoder to place the reader's held-out records. It places 42\% of the software engineer's records with the right customer (own encoder 28\%, everything 66\%). It places 31\% of the financial analyst's records with the right customer (23\% and 65\%).

Both matter, and the number matters more. Most of the gap to the encoder trained on everything comes from the records that a reader may not read at all. A smaller part comes from the fact that what they may read is mostly from one or two systems. Neither part is recoverable from company data that the reader may not read.

Knowledge from outside the company can make up some of the gap, as the backbone does for the financial analyst. Beyond that, a reader gets more only if the access policy itself gives them more. An example is a summary of each customer that everyone may read. That is a decision about access, not about the model.

\suppnote{Two channels and the memory}{sec:twochannel}
Good-view state vectors are poor at facts about which system recorded what. From a customer-quarter state, they read whether a sales call took place 72\% of the time. This is about the rate of the most common answer. Bad-view states, however, read it 97\% of the time.

Also, a store of good-view vectors answered fewer test questions than a store of generic pretrained embeddings (Methods). These two results suggest a design with two channels. We built it and tested it with the probes and with the store.

\paragraph{Facts read from states.} Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:twochannel} gives the probes that the Methods describe for single channels and for two channels side by side. A detail channel restores the facts that the state channel misses. From good views alone, probes read whether a call took place 72\% of the time. They read it 87\% of the time with the pretrained encoder as the detail channel and 95\% with bad views.

However, no combination beats the better of its two channels. Averaged over the four facts, good views reach 57\%, and the pretrained encoder reaches 63\%. Bad views reach 70\%, and good views with the pretrained encoder side by side reach 62\%. Fact by fact, the accuracy of the concatenated states is between that of their two channels, usually close to the better one. Good views read the deal stage best. Its accuracy gets worse when we add the pretrained channel and stays the same with bad views.

\paragraph{Answers from the store.} We then built the memory store with four kinds of rows and trained the same reader on each, over four seeds (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:memory2}). The four kinds are state vectors only, detail vectors only (the pretrained encoder), both channels concatenated, and both channels routed. The routed store retrieves the 16 records nearest the question in the state channel and reads their detail vectors. In every store, the 16 records come from the question's quarter. They are the records whose content in the retrieval channel is most similar to the question's (Methods).

The detail-only store answers 71.2\% of the questions, and the state-only store answers 65.8\%. The concatenated store answers 72.2\%, and the routed store answers 70.1\%. Two channels together do not clearly beat the detail channel alone. We compare two stores at a time, paired over the same questions. The concatenated store is +1.0 points (95\% interval $-$2.4 to +4.7; per seed $-$3.5, +4.2, +2.8, +0.7) ahead of the detail-only store. The detail-only store is +5.4 points (95\% interval +0.2 to +10.2; per seed +8.3, $-$2.8, +4.9, +11.1) ahead of the state-only store.

The lead does not come from the records that the stores find. With the customer named in the question, the state-only and detail-only stores retrieve the asked customer's records equally often. The asked customer's records are 65\% and 65\% of the records retrieved, and 71\% for the concatenated store.

The lead comes from how the reader reads the records, or from how we embed the question. All four kinds of question are about one system's records. The four kinds are how many tickets, the worst priority, whether a call took place and the deal stage. The detail vectors describe these records more directly. Questions that need the state channel to find the records are absent from this benchmark. The next paragraph builds a version that has them.

\paragraph{When the reader must find the customer from a record.} Every question so far names its customer. The detail channel can find the customer's records by name. We built a version in which it cannot (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:anchor}, Methods).

The question no longer names the customer. Instead, it comes with one record of the customer, its \emph{anchor}. It is as if someone who has that record asks the question. For example, someone who has a support ticket asks: was there a sales call with this customer this quarter?

The anchor comes from a thread that we hid from the state encoder's training and from the reader's pre-training. It also comes from a different system than the one that answers the question. As a result, the reader must find the customer's other records across systems from one record without its link. We ran this test in two settings. In one setting, we masked the names of customers and employees and all identifiers in every stored record and question. In the other setting, we kept them.

A store finds 16 records of the question's quarter for the reader in one of three ways. The first way takes the anchor's nearest neighbours by the content of one channel. The second way, placement, takes the records of the customer whose centroid in that channel is nearest to the anchor. In Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:heldout}, we place records at customer-quarter centroids. Here the quarter comes from the question. The third way, an oracle, takes the records of the anchor's own customer-quarter.

\textbf{With names kept, it helps most to find the records with the state channel.} Placement with the state channel finds the right customer for 66\% of the records read. It answers 63.8\% of the questions. Placement with the pretrained encoder finds the right customer for 41\% and answers 54.9\%. Paired over the same questions and anchors, the difference is +8.9 points (95\% interval +3.9 to +13.8; per seed +8.7, +11.8, +11.0, +3.9).

Nearest neighbours point the same way, but less clearly. The neighbours are from the right customer 67\% of the time for the state channel and 53\% for the detail channel. However, read through the detail channel, the state channel's neighbours answer 60.0\%, against 58.3\% for the detail channel's own neighbours.

Neighbours are records like the anchor. Placement reads a sample of the whole situation. This sample more often contains a record of the system that answers the question. With placement, 9.8\% of the records read are such records from the right customer-quarter. The share is 6.5\% for the state channel's neighbours and 3.2\% for the detail channel's neighbours.

Placement with both channels concatenated does about as well as the state channel alone (64.2\%). The best store without the oracle comes within 4.5 points of the oracle (68.7\%). It is well above no store (52.2\%). Paired with no store, placement with the state channel gains +11.6 points (95\% interval +5.1 to +18.3; per seed +11.0, +11.8, +11.0, +12.6).

\textbf{With every name masked, one record is rarely enough to find the customer.} The state channel still places held-out masked records with the right customer more often than the detail channel (33\% against 20\%). Its nearest neighbours, read through the detail channel, help a little. They answer 57.7\%, against 52.0\% with no store. The paired gain is +5.7 points (95\% interval +0.4 to +10.8; per seed +3.9, +0.8, +8.7, +9.4).

Placement does not help. It answers 52.8\%, a paired gain of +0.8 points (95\% interval $-$3.5 to +5.3; per seed $-$3.1, $-$2.4, $-$0.8, +9.4). The oracle answers 67.1\%.

\textbf{Which vectors the reader reads matters much less.} Given the oracle's records and names masked, the reader answers 69.9\% of the questions from state vectors and 67.1\% from detail vectors. With names kept, the shares are 69.9\% and 68.7\%. When the reader reads only each record's system and time codes, with no content at all, it answers 68.1\% and 67.5\%. For questions about how many tickets there were or whether a call took place, the provenance of the right records carries most of the answer.

\paragraph{What the two channels are for.} The channels do different jobs. The state channel finds the situation. It places held-out records with the right customer 76\% of the time, against 53\% for the pretrained encoder (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:heldout}). In a memory, a question can come with a record of the customer rather than a name. For such a question, it helps most to find its records through the state channel. This holds at least when we keep the names in the records.

The detail channel finds records by what they literally say, which includes names. As Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:primitives} shows, it also finds other customers in similar trouble. The state channel does not find such customers. When a store finds the right records, their provenance carries much of what questions about counts need.

It is useful to keep both channels, because a world model needs both abilities. One vector that blends them finds records about as well as the better of the two, and it answers questions no better. A memory needs to find a query's records through the state space, or by name when the query has one, before it reads them.

\suppnote{Time windows, dynamics and operational primitives}{sec:time}
\label{sec:primitives}
With quarters as windows, the company has 180 states on day~1. With all records through day~2, it has 202 states and 165 quarter-to-quarter transitions. These are too few to learn how states change. When we divide the same records by calendar month, we get 405 states on day~1. With all records through day~2, we get 469 states and 398 month-to-month transitions. ISO weeks give 1{,}445 customer-weeks with enough records to compare (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:primitives}).

\paragraph{Placement by month.} We trained a good-view encoder with customer-month states on the training threads of Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:heldout} and placed the hidden records (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:months}). The encoder places 75\% of the hidden records with the right customer and 17\% with the right customer and month. For comparison, a reference that knows the customer and chooses its busiest month gets 13\%. With quarters, good views did no better than a reference that knows the customer and chooses its busiest quarter. With months, they do somewhat better, so finer windows let the model use some of the time information in the text. The pretrained encoder, with or without a good-view head, finds the right month less often than the busiest-month reference (7\% and 9\%).

\paragraph{Forecasting and linear dynamics.} Month states do not predict the next month (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:time}). Probes that hold out one customer at a time predict next month's facts no better than the most common answer. The most common answer gets 54\% on average, against 46\% for good-view month states, 54\% for bad views and 53\% for two channels. A copy of this month's true facts does far better (72\%).

We also fitted the simplest dynamics model: a linear map from a month's state to its change into the next month (ridge regression). We compared it with a no-change prediction, and with shrinkage of the state toward the average state by one fitted factor. This shrinkage is itself a linear predictor. With a fixed, weak regularization ($\alpha = 10$), the map overfits. In the good-view space, it increases the squared error by 55\% relative to a no-change prediction.

With the regularization chosen by nested leave-one-customer-out validation, the map reduces the error in every space (by 2--31\%). But it never reduces the error by more than 3.4 points beyond what the single shrinkage factor achieves. In 4 of the six spaces, it gains at most half a point over that factor. The bad-view and pretrained spaces are noisy from month to month. So the best linear guess moves the current state about halfway toward the average (shrinkage factors 0.46 and 0.51). Good-view month states are smoother (factor 0.79, cosine 0.83 between consecutive months).

Almost everything that a linear model learns here is mean reversion. With 2.4 times as many transitions as the quarters give, linear models still learn little beyond it. Two to three times as many transitions are not enough to learn how a customer's situation moves in this data. This predictor has no actions. Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:decisions} adds the company's actions.

A shared state space can turn operational questions into geometry. We tested three such questions against Era's ground truth (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:primitives}). Methods gives the details.

\paragraph{Is something off? Drift.} For every customer and ISO week with at least three records, we computed a state vector. We scored the week by its cosine distance from the mean of the customer's previous one to four weekly states within eight weeks (1{,}445 customer-weeks). There are 279 weeks with an escalation (a new urgent or high-priority ticket) or a change of deal stage. If the state moves when the situation does, high scores should mark these weeks, or the weeks just before them. They do not, beyond noise: for every representation, the AUROC is 0.47--0.54, within about two standard errors of chance ($0.50 \pm$ 0.04). For comparison, a score that uses only the change in the number of records gets 0.57.

\paragraph{Is something off? Misalignment between teams.} We used every customer-month with at least two sales records (CRM and calls) and two support records (tickets and issues). For these 163 customer-months, we computed the cosine distance between the sales view and the support view of the customer. There are 31 months with an urgent or high-priority ticket during an open deal. If the two teams' records drift apart when a customer is at risk, the distance should be larger in these months. It is not: the AUROC is 0.48--0.57, within two standard errors of chance. With so few months at risk, two standard errors are $\pm$0.12.

\paragraph{What is this like? Similar situations across customers.} We used every customer-quarter state with at least five records (160 states). For each one, we retrieved the five most similar states of \emph{other} customers and checked whether they share its situation. We defined the situation from the ground truth: the number of tickets (four buckets) and the deal stage (open, won, lost or none). Here every representation does better than chance. With good views, 18\% of the retrieved states share the situation, and with bad views 21\%, against 8\% by chance.

The calendar alone does not explain this. When we restrict the search to other customers' states in other quarters, precision is 17\% against 7\% by chance. If we picked other customers in the same quarter, we would get 13\%.

The channels differ sharply for customers in trouble: quarters with an urgent or high-priority ticket during an open deal (22 states). Among the five most similar states of other customers, 35\% are also in trouble with bad views and 25\% with the pretrained encoder. The base rate is 12\%. But only 4\% are also in trouble with good views, and only 5\% with the two channels concatenated. Trouble, as we define it here, depends on facts about the records of particular systems (ticket priorities and deal stages). The detail channel keeps this kind of fact, and the state channel discards it.

Two of the three primitives fail on this data. The third works with every representation, and for customers in trouble only with the channels that keep detail.

We see three possible reasons for the failures, and we cannot separate them here. First, Era may generate escalations without earlier signs in the text of other systems. Second, weekly and monthly states average few records and are noisy. Third, we trained none of the encoders to register change. Good views make the state stable by design, and we do not train the other objectives on time at all.

A world model that answers ``is something off?'' will probably need a predictor trained for it, not distances in a space trained for something else. That predictor will probably need training on more transitions than one simulated company provides.

\suppnote{Decisions}{sec:decisions}
The predictor of a world model is action-conditioned, $s_{e,t+1} \approx h(s_{e,t}, a_t)$ (Methods). The dynamics of Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:time} had no actions. The records say what the company did. For each customer and month, we read six actions from the records and Era's ground truth (Methods).

In 21\% of customer-months, the company holds a sales call. In 71\%, a leader (the CEO or the head of engineering) writes to or about the customer. In 56\%, the company logs a CRM task. In 15\%, the company makes an engineering escalation (a Jira issue opened for the customer). In 43\%, support agents reply on the customer's tickets. In 78\%, employees write on the customer's email threads.

Outcomes come from the ground truth. In 17\% of months, a deal advances or the company wins it. In 25\%, there is an urgent or high-priority ticket. Actions and outcomes are associated. A deal advanced in 54\% of the months with a sales call and in 5\% of the months without one. Every comparison below is observational: the company did not randomize its actions.

\paragraph{Action-conditioned dynamics.} We added the six actions to the linear predictor of Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:time} (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:actions}, top). This month's actions do not help predict next month's state. Relative to the state alone, they change the error by $-$0.1\% to +0.6\% of the state-only error across the four representations (positive is worse). For good views, the change is +0.4\% (95\% interval $-$1.6\% to +2.3\%). The same actions, shuffled across months, cost 1.0--1.4\%. This month's mix of systems, the share of its records that each system wrote, changes the error by +0.2\% to +1.4\%.

One reason why this month's actions add nothing in the good-view and bad-view spaces is that the state already holds them. From the first 16 components of this month's state, a logistic regression predicts each of the six actions. With one customer held out at a time, its AUROC is 0.63--0.88 with good views and 0.77--0.98 with bad views. In the pretrained and two-channel spaces, the actions are harder to read (0.49--0.86 and 0.45--0.88), and they still add nothing. So redundancy is not the whole explanation.

Next month's actions do help: they lower the error by a further 9--19\% of the state-only error (good views 8.7\%, interval 6.9--10.7\%). However, they are also a description of next month's records. A Jira record is an engineering escalation, and each of the other actions is a kind of record of one system. Next month's mix of systems lowers the error by about as much on average (10--24\%), though unevenly across customers (every interval includes zero). With the mix in the model, next month's actions lower the error by a further 3--5\% (the interval excludes zero only for good views). Much of what next month's actions add is what they say about the records that we build next month's state from.

\paragraph{Outcomes.} For what happens to the customer, this month's actions do add signal, but mostly through the persistence of tickets (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:actions}, bottom). From this month's state, we predict whether a deal advances next month with AUROC 0.50--0.63 across the four representations. For an urgent ticket next month, the AUROC is 0.52--0.78. When we add this month's actions, these AUROCs change by +0.02 to +0.07 for deals and +0.02 to +0.26 for urgent tickets. The interval excludes zero in one of the four representations for deals and in three for urgent tickets. The gain is largest where the state is weakest.

The signal is in the support replies. The state plus support replies gives the same AUROC as the state plus all six actions (differences $-$0.02 to +0.02, every interval including zero). The reply indicator by itself ranks next month's urgent tickets with AUROC 0.79.

A support reply marks a month with a ticket. A support reply occurs in 99\% of the months with a ticket and in 6\% of the months without one. The indicator of any ticket this month ranks next month's urgent tickets as well (AUROC 0.79). Customers with tickets continue to have them. The reply mostly marks that persistence rather than the effect of a decision.

\paragraph{Around single actions.} At a finer scale, we studied the good-view state in the 14 days before and after an action. We compared it with the same customer's state around a time with no action of that kind within 14 days. We left all records of the action's kind out of both windows. We used only actions with at least two other records of the customer on each side. The actions are 216 sales calls (at 20 customers), 101 engineering escalations (11 customers) and 66 first leader messages after two quiet weeks (19 customers).

The state moves more around engineering escalations than at matched times. The value of 1 $-$ cosine is 0.037 against 0.026, a difference of +0.011 (interval +0.004 to +0.020). But their windows hold more records: a median of 64 before the event, against 46. When we account for the record count of each window, the difference is +0.009 (interval $-$0.003 to +0.017).

When we account for record counts, the difference around sales calls is 0.000 (interval $-$0.008 to +0.010). Around leaders' messages, it is $-$0.024 (interval $-$0.065 to +0.022), from only 29 matched times. There are few matched times because leaders write about most customers often. Information about which action the company took does not help predict where the state goes either. Across 729 pairs of windows, this information changes the error of a linear predictor by +0.02\%.

\paragraph{Decision simulation.} There are 29 customer-months in trouble, with an urgent or high-priority ticket during an open deal. For these months, we asked the question that a world model of a company is for. What follows if the company intervenes, with a sales call or an engineering escalation, and what follows if it does not (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:simulate})? The months come from eight customers, and 12 of the months come from one customer. The procedure runs as designed: the state space retrieves similar situations of other customers, splits them by what the company did, and returns what followed.

We cannot take the answers as effects, and the data show why. First, the logs barely contain the alternative: the company intervened in 25 of the 29 months and did not intervene in 4. Second, decisions follow situations that the state does not fully capture. We can predict from a month's state whether the company intervened in that month. With one customer held out at a time, the AUROC is 0.68--0.94 across the four representations.

Yet across the four spaces, the most similar states of other customers in which the company intervened were themselves in trouble 19--24\% of the time. They had an urgent ticket 57--62\% of the time. Those in which it did not intervene were in trouble 0.2--4\% of the time and had an urgent ticket 17--35\% of the time. In the good-view space, the shares are 24\% and 60\% against 0.2\% and 17\%.

The case-based comparison therefore sets troubled months against calm ones. In this comparison, more urgent tickets appear to follow when the company intervenes (+29 points, interval +6 to +45). This is what we would see if the company makes calls and escalations when problems are severe, whatever their effect.

The estimates also move with the space that we use to find similar states. They are +12 to +15 points for deals and +8 to +29 for urgent tickets, and six of the eight intervals include zero. The outcome model, which takes the decision as an input, attributes little to it (0 and +5 points). Within the months in trouble, the first call or escalation came before the month's first urgent ticket in 13 and after it in 12. So a monthly action indicator mixes responses to a ticket with actions that the company took before it.

We can thus build the decision layer of an operational world model on the state space from the company's own records, without new data. Its parts are actions, action-conditioned transitions, event windows and decision simulation.

On one company's logs, this layer learns little about what decisions do. This month's actions add nothing to next month's state, and in the good-view space the state already encodes most of them. Next month's actions help mostly as a description of the records that we build next month's state from. Once we account for record counts, no single action moves the state detectably more than matched times. The company intervened in situations unlike those in which it did not intervene. When we matched on the state, these differences did not disappear in any of the four spaces.

Learning from decisions will need actions with a known assignment. Such actions are decisions logged with their reasons, deliberately varied, or replayed in a simulator that can run the same situation with a different decision.

\suppnote{What a shared state space is for}{sec:whatfor}
A shared state space turns everyday questions into geometry.
\emph{Where are we?} The state of a customer this week is a point. Its nearest neighbours from every system are the records that matter. The encoder can also place records that nobody linked, which are 72\% of all records in our simulated company. To test this, we hide links (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:heldout}).

\emph{What changed?} The difference between this week's state and last week's.
\emph{What is this like?} Situations that look alike across customers, such as accounts that look the way a lost account looked before the company lost it.
\emph{Is something off?} A state that moves fast, or two teams whose records about the same customer drift apart.

\emph{What about new tools and new models?} In our experiments, one rotation fitted on 64 matched pairs of records can partly align separately trained encoders. A retrained encoder can also search the old index through another rotation (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:e2}).
We test the second, third and fourth of the questions above directly in Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:time}. On this data, only the third has an answer yet.

For an individual, the difference is concrete. A new account manager sees one customer's situation assembled from systems that they never opened. A support engineer sees that the ticket in front of them belongs to a customer whose renewal call went badly yesterday. An executive asks what changed across all customers this week. The executive gets an answer computed from states, and nobody reads thousands of records to get it. AI agents read a compact, shared state and do not rebuild context from records for every request.

\suppnote{Related work}{sec:related}
\paragraph{Linked records as positives.} It is an established practice to treat records that share a link as positive pairs. Examples are citations\citep{cohan2020specter}, authors and venues\citep{zhang2022metadata}, product identifiers\citep{peeters2022supervised} and foreign keys in relational databases\citep{peleska2025relational}. Other examples are one bank client's transactions, locations and support calls\citep{mollaev2025banking}, and one patient's codes and notes\citep{cai2024ehr}. In industry, examples are per-company embedding models fine-tuned on document links, co-access and chat threads.\footnote{\url{https://jxnl.co/writing/2025/03/06/fine-tuning-embedding-models-for-enterprise-rag-lessons-from-glean/}}

The closest of these is the multimodal work on bank clients. It trains one encoder per modality jointly with a CLIP-style objective\citep{radford2021clip}. We differ in the following ways. We define states by entity \emph{and} time window across a company's systems. We train per-system encoders separately and join them by a rotation. We test the predictions of the Platonic Representation Theorem against bad-view and next-token controls.

Where the industrial practice fine-tunes a pretrained sentence encoder\citep{reimers2019sbert}, we also train only a head on a frozen one. This keeps the backbone free of company data, and therefore different access scopes can share it.

\paragraph{JEPAs for text.} LLM-JEPA applies a JEPA loss to curated pairs of text and code that describe the same thing\citep{huang2025llmjepa}. In addition, \citet{zuo2026asymmetric} apply JEPA with SIGReg to sections of a scientific paper. Our views come from organizational links. We train our encoder from scratch on one company.

\paragraph{Convergence and alignment of representations.} Beyond the Platonic hypothesis and theorem\citep{huh2024platonic,balestriero2026platonic}, anchors and orthogonal maps join independently trained latent spaces\citep{moschella2023relative,maiorca2023latent} and data silos\citep{nosaka2024collaboration}. Orthogonal Procrustes aligned word embeddings across decades\citep{hamilton2016diachronic}. Theory relates self-supervised representations to the spectrum of the positive-pair graph\citep{haochen2021spectral} and to the content that views share\citep{vonkugelgen2021content}.

\paragraph{Compatible embeddings across model updates.} Backward-compatible training makes a new model's embeddings comparable with an old index\citep{shen2020bct}. Fitted Procrustes maps or affine adapters do so after training\citep{maystre2025procrustes,vejendla2025drift}. Our rotation registry is an instance of the latter. For good-view encoders, it preserves the leading directions of the space but not the tail.

\paragraph{Access control in machine learning.} Information flow control for machine learning trains one expert module per security domain and combines only the experts that a user may access. This gives non-interference for language models\citep{tiwari2024ifc}. Permissioned LLMs attach parameter-efficient adapters to security domains and measure leakage with an access-advantage metric\citep{jayaraman2025permissioned}. A position paper argues that enterprise models must train and retrieve only on content that every participant in an interaction may access\citep{bhatt2025participant}. Permission-aware retrieval filters search results by the reader's rights. It is standard industry practice.

We bring these ideas to a learned world model of an organization. In such a model, a third leak path appears: the aggregation of records into states. We also measure what readers who may read little can recover from shared knowledge while non-interference holds.

\paragraph{Memory for language models.} Memorilla compresses a document collection into a few query-dependent memory tokens for a frozen model\citep{fallahpour2026memorilla}. xRAG compresses a retrieved document into one token\citep{cheng2024xrag}.

\paragraph{Simulated organizations and enterprise world models.} Era by Eon\citep{gruenbaum2026era}, OrgForge\citep{flynt2026orgforge}, TheAgentCompany\citep{xu2024theagentcompany} and CRMArena\citep{huang2024crmarena} simulate companies to evaluate agents. We use one of them to learn representations. \citet{nair2026enterprise} study learned world models of enterprise systems as predictors of state transitions. They find that these models falter when a deployment's configuration changes.

Organizational memory has a long history in management research\citep{walsh1991organizational}. Object-centric process mining relates events to the business objects that they touch\citep{vanderaalst2019object}. For individuals, general user models come from a person's computer use and take the form of text statements\citep{shaikh2025gum}.

\suppnote{Scope and limitations}{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One simulated company.} All results come from one Era company with 21 active customers. Era's text is cleaner and more regular than real company text, and its systems agree by construction. Replication on more simulated companies, and on real data, is the most important next step. Several negative results (drift, misalignment, forecasting) may reflect how Era generates events rather than what a state space can do. If escalations have no precursors in the text of other systems, no representation of that text can anticipate them.
\item \textbf{Links as supervision.} Links are not annotations, but they are supervision. The positive pairs come from the same customer-quarter groups that most of our metrics score. Results on training records (Supplementary Notes~\ref{sec:e1} and~\ref{sec:mask}) are therefore upper bounds. The held-out test (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:heldout}) is the fairer measure.
\item \textbf{Held-out split.} We held out whole threads, not whole periods or customers. A split by period would test more strictly whether the space learns recurring templates.
\item \textbf{Baselines trained from scratch.} With our small encoder, corruption-based views and next-token prediction learn little about customers on training records. There, they do no better than a random encoder. The pretrained encoder is a stronger baseline, and good views improve it too. However, we tested only one small, frozen backbone. A larger encoder, fine-tuned end to end, may behave differently.
\item \textbf{Seeds.} The main comparison uses three seeds, and the memory reader uses four. The held-out, alignment, time, masking, noise, pretrained, access-control, primitive and decision experiments use one seed.
\item \textbf{Partial alignment.} Separately trained encoders agree on the leading directions, not on the tail. Messages and systems of record share only part of the state.
\item \textbf{The question benchmark.} The 144 test questions ask only about counts and categories within one system's records. We constructed the anchored version so that it needs the state channel. Its anchors come from held-out threads, and from systems other than the one that answers the question. Retrieval takes the quarter from the question. We dropped the questions without such an anchor (127 of 144 test questions remain).

The four seeds of the anchored version vary the reader and the anchors but not the state encoder, which is one run. We did not test questions that need several systems' records at once, or that come with several records of the customer.
\item \textbf{Dynamics.} With quarterly or monthly windows, forecasts do not beat the most common answer, and a tuned linear predictor beats a simple shrinkage toward the mean by at most 3.4 points (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:time}). The company's logged actions do not change this (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:decisions}). Drift and misalignment scores show no signal beyond noise (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:primitives}).
\item \textbf{Actions read from logs with simple rules, and observed rather than assigned.} We read the six actions with simple rules. Under these rules, a support agent is anyone who comments on the tickets of at least three customers. Leaders are the CEO and the head of engineering. The company did not randomize its decisions, so the decision simulation estimates what followed each decision, not its effect. The 29 months in trouble come from eight customers (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:decisions}).
\item \textbf{Access-control policy.} We built our role-based policy from what our export kept: system, channel privacy, direct-message membership, sender and organizer. Real deployments have finer rules: channel membership, email recipients, internal notes and record sharing. These rules plug into the same mechanism, but we did not test them. Per-scope models also cost one training run per scope, and very small scopes may not have enough records to train one. A shared backbone helps some of them (Supplementary Note~\ref{sec:acl-results}).
\end{itemize}
