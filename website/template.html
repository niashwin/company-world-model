Every company is a world. We build its world model.

A company's email, chat, CRM and support systems each see one part of every customer, deal and project. A world model learns the whole: how each situation stands, how it changes, and who may see what. It learns the way a new employee does, from the company's own records, with no labels.

The method comes from world models of the physical world, such as Yann LeCun's JEPA. A 2026 proof by Randall Balestriero and Yann LeCun explains why it works. In a simulated company, our world model placed {{HOGOODCUST}} of records whose link it never saw with the right customer. A keyword search placed {{HOTFCUST}}.

The idea in three steps

  1. Step 1

    A company is a world of its own

    Every customer, deal and project has a state: how things stand right now. Is the customer happy? Is the deal at risk? Nobody sees this state directly. Each person and each tool sees only a part of it.

  2. Step 2

    Every system is a view of that world

    An email, a support ticket and a CRM update about one customer in one week are three views of one situation. The systems already link many records to their customer. These links are free training data, and nobody has to label anything.

  3. Step 3

    Predict one view from another, and the state appears

    World models learn about the physical world this way. Yann LeCun's JEPA learns to predict one view of a scene from another view of it. Randall Balestriero and Yann LeCun proved that, with the right pairs of views, such models converge to the same picture of the true state. We give the model pairs of linked records, and it learns the state of the company.

Why this is fundamental

World models changed how AI understands the physical world. They learn from prediction, not from labels, and one model then serves perception, prediction and planning. V-JEPA 2 learns the state of a scene from video, and then plans the actions of a robot.

A company is a world too, and its systems record it from every side. A world model turns those records into one picture of every customer, deal and project. Search, memory, permissions and prediction can then all draw on the same picture.

The theory also says what matters most: which records count as views of the same situation. Companies already record those pairs as a side effect of their work.

We test the idea on Northwind Robotics, a company that Era by Eon simulates with exact ground truth. It has {{CORPUSRECORDS}} records in seven systems, and {{CORPUSCUSTOMERS}} customers. With a simulation, we can check every answer against the truth.

The foundation for corporate AI

Today, AI for companies comes one capability at a time: search, a memory, notes, and a permission filter for each. Each tool builds its own partial picture of the company. A world model is one picture of the whole company, and every capability can draw on it.

Each capability then inherits the permissions of the world model. Each one also gains what a store of documents cannot give: a model of how situations change.

What the world model already does

Eight results from a simulated company with exact ground truth. Each one says what we tested, what happened, and what it could mean for a real company.

  • {{HOGOODCUST}}

    Records find their customer, even without their link

    We hid the customer links of {{HONTEST}} records and asked the world model where each one belongs. It put {{HOGOODCUST}} with the right customer. A keyword search put {{HOTFCUST}} there. A guess of the largest customer gets {{HOMAJCUST}}.

    Why it matters. AI can assemble one picture of each customer from every tool, also from records that lost their link.

    How we measured it

  • {{ANCHMHOSTATE}}

    It recognizes customers without their names

    We removed every name, email address and identifier from the records. The world model still put {{ANCHMHOSTATE}} of the hidden records with the right customer. A small ready-made AI text model (MiniLM) put {{ANCHMHODETAIL}} there, and a guess of the largest customer gets {{HOMAJCUST}}.

    Why it matters. Names do much of the work, but not all of it. The world model also learns what each customer's situation looks like.

    How we measured it

  • {{PTRAWHO}} → {{PTHEADHO}}

    It also improves a ready-made AI text model

    We added the same training on top of a small ready-made AI text model, MiniLM. Its share of hidden records with the right customer rose from {{PTRAWHO}} to {{PTHEADHO}}.

    Why it matters. A company can add this training to a model that it already runs, and does not have to replace the model. We tested one small model. Larger models are a next step.

    How we measured it

  • No leak

    Privacy is part of the world model

    We planted secret codenames in {{ACLCANARYN}} records that two employees may not read, and searched for them as each employee. A model trained on everything pointed to the right customer every time, even when we filtered the results to what the employee may read. World models trained only on what each employee may read gave the same results with and without the secrets.

    Why it matters. A permission filter on search results is not enough. When each group's world model learns only from what the group may read, it cannot reveal the rest. People who may read little get less accurate results.

    How we measured it

  • {{ANCHPPLACESTATEACC}}

    One email can stand in for the customer's name

    We asked questions that came with one email or ticket instead of the customer's name. With the world model, the system gave the right answer {{ANCHPPLACESTATEACC}} of the time. With the small ready-made model it did {{ANCHPPLACEDETAILACC}}, and with no memory {{ANCHPCLOSEDACC}}. A system that knows the email's true link gets {{ANCHPLINKACC}}. The names stayed in the records for this test.

    Why it matters. People rarely ask with exact names. A forwarded email or a ticket can bring the right customer's records.

    How we measured it

  • {{SITRANGE}}

    “Have we seen this before?” gets a first answer

    For each customer and quarter, we found the five most similar quarters of other customers. Of these, {{SITRANGE}} shared the situation, against {{SITCHANCE}} by chance. The share depends on the representation.

    Why it matters. A team can find earlier cases like the one in front of it. This is a first, rough answer, at about twice chance.

    How we measured it

  • {{SIMINTERV}} of {{SIMTROUBLE}}

    Logs show what a company did, not what worked

    We studied {{SIMTROUBLE}} months in which a customer was in trouble. The company stepped in {{SIMINTERV}} times. So the logs hold almost no cases in which it did not act, and they cannot show which actions help.

    Why it matters. To predict what a decision will do, a world model needs decisions with their reasons, and some decisions that vary on purpose. A company can start to record them now.

    How we measured it

  • {{TRAINMINLO}}–{{TRAINMINHI}}min

    It is cheap to train

    Each model that we timed trained in {{TRAINMINLO}} to {{TRAINMINHI}} minutes on an ordinary computer, without a GPU.

    Why it matters. With {{CORPUSLINKED}} linked records, as here, a company can train the model again often. It can also keep a separate model for each group of people with the same access. We did not test a company with millions of records.

    How to run it

The road to corporate AI

We call the goal corporate AI: AI that understands how a company works, acts within each person's permissions, and helps the company decide. A world model of the company is at the center of it.

  1. 1

    Memory that accumulates

    A memory of the company keeps what happened, who decided it and why, and where each fact came from. Products such as Sentra build this layer today.

    In place
  2. 2

    A world model of the company

    One learned picture of every customer, deal and project, from the links between all of the company's systems, with permissions built in. This study builds a first one, in a simulated company.

    Shown in simulation
  3. 3

    Prediction and decisions

    The world model predicts how situations change and what decisions do. People and AI agents test a decision against it before they act. A first version runs here.

    First version
  4. 4

    An organization that learns

    Decisions and their outcomes become new records, and the world model learns from each one. When some decisions vary on purpose, it learns what they do.

    Future

This is a first world model of a company. It will improve with real data, larger models and records of decisions. Search, memory, permissions and prediction can all build on it, and that is why we start here.

The results in detail

This part is for readers who want the evidence. Each section shows one experiment, its numbers and an interactive figure. The paper gives every detail.

The state of the company, from seven systems

For a company: one picture of each customer across every tool, also for a record without its link.

A world model has two parts. Its state space places every situation, and its predictor tells how situations change. This section tests the state space.

An encoder is a model that turns each record into a point in the state space. We train small encoders from scratch on the linked records, with three objectives on the same text. Good views pair two records of the same customer and quarter, from any system. Bad views pair a record with a damaged copy of itself. Next-token prediction learns to predict the next word, as most language models do.

First, we measure on the training records. With good views, {{E1GOODPCT}} of a record's ten nearest records from other systems belong to the same customer. Bad views and next-token prediction reach {{E1BADPCT}} and {{E1NTPPCT}}.

The fairer test hides the links of some records. The encoder never sees their links, and their threads are not in the training data. A good-view encoder places {{HOGOODCUST}} of these records with the right customer. A keyword search (TF-IDF) places {{HOTFCUST}}, and a guess of the largest customer {{HOMAJCUST}}. The same good views also raise a frozen pretrained encoder (MiniLM) from {{PTRAWHO}} to {{PTHEADHO}}.

The space learns more than names. With every name and identifier masked, it still places {{ANCHMHOSTATE}} of these records with the right customer, against {{ANCHMHODETAIL}} for the pretrained encoder. It finds the customer more sharply than the exact quarter, and monthly windows add some timing.

Access control, built in

For a company: each person's model learns only from what that person may read, so it cannot reveal the rest.

A learned world model can leak through three paths: search results, states that combine records a reader may not open, and the encoder's own training. Filters on search results and states close only the first two paths.

To test this, we plant secrets in {{ACLCANARYN}} records that a reader may not read. Then the reader searches for them. With an encoder trained on everything, the search still points to the right customer, even when we filter the search and the states.

When each reader's model trains only on the records that the reader may read, every result is the same with and without the secrets. The cost falls on people who may read little. A shared pretrained backbone, which holds no company data, recovers part of that cost for some of them.

Two channels: state and detail

For a company: a question that starts from a ticket or an email, not a name, more often reaches the right customer's records.

The state channel tells where a record belongs: which customer and which situation. The detail channel tells what the record says. A pretrained sentence encoder keeps these details. A memory of the company needs both channels.

We ask questions about customers, for example how many tickets a customer had in a quarter. A small reader answers from 16 records that a store finds. The reader is a small version of the Memorilla design.

When a question names the customer, a store of pretrained-encoder vectors, the detail channel, answers as well as any combination of channels.

Sometimes a question comes with one record of the customer instead of the name. We call this record the anchor. A store first places the anchor with a customer, and the reader then reads that customer's records. With the names kept, placement with the state channel answers {{ANCHPPLACESTATEACC}} of the questions, against {{ANCHPPLACEDETAILACC}} with the pretrained encoder and {{ANCHPCLOSEDACC}} with no store. With every name masked, the gain is smaller.

Prediction is the next layer

For a company: prediction is the next step. It needs a model trained to forecast, and data about decisions.

A world model must also predict how a situation changes. On this company's logs, our predictors learn little beyond one rule: next month looks like this month, a little closer to the average. A tuned linear predictor does at most {{TUNEDMAXGAIN}} points better than that rule.

The space already answers a related question: what is this like? Every representation finds similar situations of other customers at about twice chance.

Decisions: what the company did, and what followed

For a company: to learn what works, log decisions with their reasons, and vary some of them on purpose.

We read six kinds of action from the records: sales calls, messages from leaders, CRM tasks, engineering escalations, support replies and emails to customers. Then we add the actions to the predictor.

This month's actions add little to the prediction of next month's state. One reason is that the state already reflects most of them.

We also simulate a decision for {{SIMTROUBLE}} customer-months in trouble: an urgent or high-priority ticket during an open deal. For each month, we find similar months of other customers. Then we compare the months in which the company intervened with the months in which it did not intervene.

The company intervened in {{SIMINTERV}} of the {{SIMTROUBLE}} months. The similar months in which it intervened were in trouble {{SIMBALTROUBLEONR}} of the time, against {{SIMBALTROUBLEOFFR}} for the months in which it did not intervene. So the comparison sets troubled months against calm months. It shows where the company acted, not what the action did. To learn the effect of a decision, the data must record why each decision happened. Similar cases must also get different decisions (Pearl; Hernán and Robins).

What comes next

This first world model of a company already finds, files and compares situations. It grows in three directions.

  1. Real companies. Real companies have more customers, more systems and noisier text than the simulation. The method needs only the links that their systems already keep.
  2. Decisions with known effects. The decisive test needs a simulator with a hidden state and known effects of actions. There, one customer can face different decisions: an engineering escalation, a discount or a call from an executive. A world model passes the test if it ranks those decisions by their true effects for customers that it never saw. It must also beat a memory with simple rules.
  3. Several timescales. Tickets move in hours, deals in weeks and strategy in quarters. A hierarchy of state spaces, one for each timescale, extends this work in the manner of H-JEPA.

Scope of this study

  • All results come from one simulated company, whose text is cleaner than real company text.
  • The company's decisions come from its logs. So the decision layer describes what followed each decision, not its effect.
  • We used small encoders. Most results come from one run. The main comparison uses three runs, and the memory reader four.

Reproduce the work

Everything runs on a CPU. Each encoder and head that we timed trains in {{TRAINMINLO}}–{{TRAINMINHI}} minutes. The paper and its figures rebuild from the stored results in about a minute. Every number in the paper comes from these results.

# the paper and its figures, from results/ (no data needed)
git clone {{CODEURL}}
cd company-world-model
pip install -r requirements.txt
make all
pytest -q tests

# every experiment, from the simulated company to the paper (several hours, Docker for Era)
bash scripts/reproduce.sh

Era by Eon generates the company. The repository does not include the generated data. The script starts the seven simulators with Docker and exports every record. See REPRODUCE.md for the command that makes each table.
