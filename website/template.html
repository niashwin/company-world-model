Every system sees the same company

Toward world models of organizations

Ashwin Gopinath, Sentra, San Francisco. November 2026.

A company keeps its records in many systems: email, chat, the CRM, support tickets, call recordings, calendars and issue trackers. Each system records a part of what happens with a customer. No system records the situation itself.

We learn that situation from the links that the systems already keep. The result is one state space for the whole company, with access control built in. We test it on a simulated company with exact ground truth.

The idea

A world model of an organization has a state space. Each point in this space is a situation, for example one customer in one quarter. Each record of the company lands at the situation that it describes, whichever system wrote it.

The Platonic Representation Theorem gives a way to learn such a space. It is about joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs), which learn to predict one view of a state from another view of the same state. The views must be good views: two different observations of the same state. With good views, such encoders converge to the same representation, up to a rotation.

Companies already keep good views. The CRM, the email system and the ticket system each link records to customers. Two records that two systems link to the same customer in the same quarter observe the same situation. Nobody has to label anything.

We test this idea on Northwind Robotics, a company that Era by Eon simulates with exact ground truth. The company has {{CORPUSRECORDS}} records in seven systems. Of these, {{CORPUSLINKED}} have a link to one of {{CORPUSCUSTOMERS}} customers. The other {{CORPUSUNLINKED}} have no link, so the encoder must place them from their content alone. We cannot score these records directly, because they have no ground truth.

One state space for seven systems

We train small text encoders from scratch on the linked records, with three objectives on the same text. Good views pair two records of the same customer and quarter, from any system. Bad views pair a record with a damaged copy of itself. Next-token prediction learns to predict the next word.

First, we measure on the training records. With good views, {{E1GOODPCT}} of a record's ten nearest records from other systems belong to the same customer. Bad views and next-token prediction reach {{E1BADPCT}} and {{E1NTPPCT}}.

The fairer test hides the links of some records. The encoder never sees their links, and their threads are not in the training data. A good-view encoder places {{HOGOODCUST}} of these records with the right customer. TF-IDF places {{HOTFCUST}}. The same good views also raise a frozen pretrained encoder from {{PTRAWHO}} to {{PTHEADHO}}.

Access control, built in

A learned world model can leak through three paths: search results, states that combine records a reader may not open, and the encoder's own training. Filters on search results and states close only the first two paths.

To test this, we plant secrets in {{ACLCANARYN}} records that a reader may not read. Then the reader searches for them. With an encoder trained on everything, the search finds the secrets, even when we filter the search and the states.

When each reader's model trains only on the records that the reader may read, every result is identical with and without the secrets. A pretrained backbone holds no company data, so all readers can share it. It helps some readers who may read little, but not all. With a head on the backbone, the financial analyst's placement rises from {{ACLCOSTFINOWN}} to {{ACL2FINHEAD}}. The software engineer's placement changes only from {{ACLCOSTENGOWN}} to {{ACL2ENGHEAD}}.

Two channels: state and detail

The state channel tells where a record belongs: which customer and which situation. The detail channel tells what the record says. A pretrained sentence encoder keeps these details. A memory of the company needs both channels.

We ask questions about customers, for example how many tickets a customer had in a quarter. A small reader answers from 16 records that a store finds. The reader follows Memorilla.

When a question names the customer, a store of pretrained-encoder vectors, the detail channel, answers as well as any combination of channels.

Sometimes a question comes with one record of the customer instead of the name. We call this record the anchor. Then a store must first place the anchor with a customer, and the reader reads that customer's records. With the names kept in the records, placement with the state channel answers {{ANCHPPLACESTATEACC}} of the questions. Placement with both channels side by side answers {{ANCHPPLACECONCATACC}}, placement with the pretrained encoder {{ANCHPPLACEDETAILACC}}, and no store {{ANCHPCLOSEDACC}}. When we mask every name in the records, only the anchor's own link helps clearly.

Time and dynamics

A world model must also predict how a situation changes. Here our result is negative. With monthly and weekly windows, forecasts, linear dynamics, drift scores and distances between teams stay at chance or at simple baselines.

A linear predictor of next month's state does at most {{TUNEDMAXGAIN}} points better than a simple shrinkage toward the mean. In {{TUNEDNWORSE}} of the 6 spaces, it gains at most half a point. Almost all that it learns is a return toward the mean. Every representation finds similar situations of other customers at about twice chance, and good views have no advantage here.

Decisions: what the company did, and what followed

We read six kinds of action from the records: sales calls, messages from leaders, CRM tasks, engineering escalations, support replies and emails to customers. Then we add the actions to the predictor.

This month's actions do not help to predict next month's state. The error changes by {{ACTLAGRANGE}} of the error without the actions. In the good-view space, this month's state already predicts most of the actions, with an AUROC of {{ACTFROMSTATEGOOD}}.

We also simulate a decision for {{SIMTROUBLE}} customer-months in trouble: an urgent or high-priority ticket during an open deal. For each month, we find similar months of other customers. Then we compare the months in which the company intervened with the months in which it did not intervene.

The company intervened in {{SIMINTERV}} of the {{SIMTROUBLE}} months. The similar months in which it intervened were in trouble {{SIMBALTROUBLEONR}} of the time, against {{SIMBALTROUBLEOFFR}} for the months in which it did not intervene. So the comparison sets troubled months against calm months. It shows where the company acted, not what the action did.

Toward an operational world model

Much of enterprise AI today is retrieval. Each question rebuilds the situation from documents, and then the system forgets it. A world model keeps the state of the organization up to date as records arrive. People and agents then ask the state, not the documents.

An operational world model has four parts, and each part needs the one before it:

  1. A shared state space, learned from the links that the systems keep. Shown here
  2. A memory that keeps the state and the detail of every record, and answers each question from the right channel. Shown in part
  3. A predictor of how states change, trained on the company's own history of states, decisions and outcomes. Not yet
  4. Planning: people and agents test a decision against the predictor before they make it. Future work

The state space trains in minutes on a CPU. At this scale, a company could retrain it and the model of each access scope every day, or when an access rule changes.

Current limits

  • All results come from one simulated company. Its text is cleaner than real text, and its systems agree by construction. Some negative results may come from how Era generates events.
  • Links are a weak form of supervision, and most metrics score the same groups that the links define. So results on training records are upper limits. The test on records with hidden links is the fairer measure.
  • We tested one small, frozen pretrained backbone. A larger encoder that we fine-tune end to end may behave differently.
  • The main comparison uses {{E1GOODNSEEDS}} seeds and the memory reader 4. Most other experiments use one run.
  • The question benchmark is narrow. Its 144 test questions ask only about counts and categories in the records of one system.
  • Forecasts do not beat the most common answer. A tuned linear predictor beats a simple shrinkage toward the mean by at most {{TUNEDMAXGAIN}} points. The company's logged actions do not change this.
  • We read actions from the logs with simple rules, and the company did not assign them at random. So the simulation shows what followed each decision, not its effect.

Reproduce the work

Everything runs on a CPU. Each encoder and head that we timed trains in {{TRAINMINLO}}–{{TRAINMINHI}} minutes. The paper and its figures rebuild from the stored results in about a minute. Every number in the paper comes from these results.

# the paper and its figures, from results/ (no data needed)
git clone {{CODEURL}}
cd company-world-model
pip install -r requirements.txt
make all
pytest -q tests

# every experiment, from the simulated company to the paper (several hours, Docker for Era)
bash scripts/reproduce.sh

Era by Eon generates the company. The repository does not include the generated data. The script starts the seven simulators with Docker and exports every record. See REPRODUCE.md in the repository for the command that makes each table.
