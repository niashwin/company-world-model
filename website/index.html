Every system sees the same company

Toward world models of organizations

Ashwin Gopinath, Sentra, San Francisco. November 2026.

A company keeps its records in many systems: email, chat, the CRM, support tickets, call recordings, calendars and issue trackers. Each system records a part of what happens with a customer. No system records the situation itself.

We learn that situation from the links that the systems already keep. The result is one state space for the whole company, with permissions built in. It is the first learned layer of a world model of the organization. We test it on a simulated company with exact ground truth.

What this means for corporate AI

At Sentra, we call it corporate AI: AI that understands how a company works, acts within its permissions and helps it decide.

Many AI assistants rebuild context from documents for each question, and then forget it. Two questions about the same customer can rebuild the situation differently. Corporate AI needs a model of the company itself, which stays current as the company works. We build it in four layers. Each layer is useful on its own, and each one makes the next one possible.

  1. 1

    Memory that accumulates

    An organizational memory keeps what happened, who decided it and why, and where each fact came from. Sentra builds this kind of memory today.

    In place
  2. 2

    Representations that learn

    One state space for the whole company, learned from the links between its systems. It places each record with its customer, and permissions are part of it. This is the work on this page.

    Shown in simulation
  3. 3

    Models that predict

    A predictor of how situations change, and of what decisions do. We build a first version on the state space, and we show what data it needs to learn the effects of decisions.

    Next
  4. 4

    Organizations that improve

    People and agents test a decision against the model before they act. Then they compare what the model predicted with what happened, and the model learns from the difference.

The idea

A world model of an organization has a state space. Each point in this space is a situation, for example one customer in one quarter. Each record of the company lands at the situation that it describes, whichever system wrote it.

A recent result, the Platonic Representation Theorem, shows how to learn such a space. A model learns to predict one record of a situation from another record of the same situation. Such pairs are good views: two different observations of the same state. With good views, models of this kind (JEPAs) converge to the same space, up to a rotation.

Companies already keep good views. The CRM, the email system and the ticket system each link records to customers. Two records that two systems link to the same customer in the same quarter observe the same situation. Nobody has to label anything.

We test this idea on Northwind Robotics, a company that Era by Eon simulates with exact ground truth. The company has 78,048 records in seven systems. Of these, 21,972 have a link to one of 21 customers. The other 72% have no link, so the encoder must place them from their content alone. We cannot score these records directly, because they have no ground truth.

One state space for seven systems

For a company: one picture of each customer across every tool, also for a record without its link.

We train small text encoders from scratch on the linked records, with three objectives on the same text. Good views pair two records of the same customer and quarter, from any system. Bad views pair a record with a damaged copy of itself. Next-token prediction learns to predict the next word.

First, we measure on the training records. With good views, 95.1% of a record's ten nearest records from other systems belong to the same customer. Bad views and next-token prediction reach 13.6% and 13.2%.

The fairer test hides the links of some records. The encoder never sees their links, and their threads are not in the training data. A good-view encoder places 76% of these records with the right customer. TF-IDF places 53%, and a guess of the largest customer 12%. The same good views also raise a frozen pretrained encoder from 53% to 62%.

The space learns more than names. With every name and identifier masked, it still places 33% of these records with the right customer, against 20% for the pretrained encoder. It finds the customer more sharply than the exact quarter, and monthly windows add some timing.

Access control, built in

For a company: each person's model learns only from what that person may read, so it cannot reveal the rest.

A learned world model can leak through three paths: search results, states that combine records a reader may not open, and the encoder's own training. Filters on search results and states close only the first two paths.

To test this, we plant secrets in 150 records that a reader may not read. Then the reader searches for them. With an encoder trained on everything, the search finds the secrets, even when we filter the search and the states.

When each reader's model trains only on the records that the reader may read, every result is the same with and without the secrets. The cost falls on people who may read little. A shared pretrained backbone, which holds no company data, recovers part of that cost for some of them.

Two channels: state and detail

For a company: a question that starts from a ticket or an email, not a name, more often reaches the right customer's records.

The state channel tells where a record belongs: which customer and which situation. The detail channel tells what the record says. A pretrained sentence encoder keeps these details. A memory of the company needs both channels.

We ask questions about customers, for example how many tickets a customer had in a quarter. A small reader answers from 16 records that a store finds. The reader is a small version of the Memorilla design.

When a question names the customer, a store of pretrained-encoder vectors, the detail channel, answers as well as any combination of channels.

Sometimes a question comes with one record of the customer instead of the name. We call this record the anchor. A store first places the anchor with a customer, and the reader then reads that customer's records. With the names kept, placement with the state channel answers 63.8% of the questions, against 54.9% with the pretrained encoder and 52.2% with no store. With every name masked, the gain is smaller.

Prediction is the next layer

For a company: prediction is the next step. It needs a model trained to forecast, and data about decisions.

A world model must also predict how a situation changes. On this company's logs, our predictors learn little beyond one rule: next month looks like this month, a little closer to the average. A tuned linear predictor does at most 3.4 points better than that rule.

The space already answers a related question: what is this like? Every representation finds similar situations of other customers at about twice chance.

Decisions: what the company did, and what followed

For a company: to learn what works, log decisions with their reasons, and vary some of them on purpose.

We read six kinds of action from the records: sales calls, messages from leaders, CRM tasks, engineering escalations, support replies and emails to customers. Then we add the actions to the predictor.

This month's actions add little to the prediction of next month's state, because the state already reflects most of them.

We also simulate a decision for 29 customer-months in trouble: an urgent or high-priority ticket during an open deal. For each month, we find similar months of other customers. Then we compare the months in which the company intervened with the months in which it did not intervene.

The company intervened in 25 of the 29 months. The similar months in which it intervened were in trouble 19–24% of the time, against 0.2–4% for the months in which it did not intervene. So the comparison sets troubled months against calm months. It shows where the company acted, not what the action did.

How this compares

Enterprise AI gives models knowledge of a company in several ways. They are layers of one architecture more than rivals. Most of them keep or read what the company wrote. This work adds the layer that organizes those records into situations before anything reads them.

What comes next

The decisive next test is causal. It needs a simulator with a hidden state and known effects of actions. There, one customer can face different decisions: an engineering escalation, a discount or a call from an executive. A world model passes the test if it ranks those decisions by their true effects for customers that it never saw. It must also beat a memory with simple rules.

Organizations also change at several speeds. Tickets move in hours, deals in weeks and strategy in quarters. A hierarchy of state spaces, one for each timescale, is a direct extension of this work.

Scope of this study

  • All results come from one simulated company, whose text is cleaner than real company text.
  • The company's decisions come from its logs. So the decision layer describes what followed each decision, not its effect.
  • We used small encoders. Most results come from one run. The main comparison uses three runs, and the memory reader four.

Reproduce the work

Everything runs on a CPU. Each encoder and head that we timed trains in 2–7 minutes. The paper and its figures rebuild from the stored results in about a minute. Every number in the paper comes from these results.

# the paper and its figures, from results/ (no data needed)
git clone https://github.com/niashwin/company-world-model
cd company-world-model
pip install -r requirements.txt
make all
pytest -q tests

# every experiment, from the simulated company to the paper (several hours, Docker for Era)
bash scripts/reproduce.sh

Era by Eon generates the company. The repository does not include the generated data. The script starts the seven simulators with Docker and exports every record. See REPRODUCE.md in the repository for the command that makes each table.
